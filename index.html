Module 1 · Week 1

Business domain & where VibeIQ sits

The foundation week. Light on mechanics by design — you can't reason about integrations until you understand what the platform is for, whose calendar it runs on, and what problem it solves for whom.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can (1) describe the seasonal go-to-market process at the level a merchandiser would recognize, using their vocabulary, (2) explain in one minute what VibeIQ does and why it is not a PLM, (3) draw a customer's system landscape and say which system owns which attribute, and (4) navigate the "Contrail vs. marketing brand" distinction.

Concept 1 — The domain: seasonal product creation

VibeIQ's customers are brands and retailers that create seasonal, visual, assortment-driven product — apparel, footwear, accessories, home, hardlines, toys, beauty. The defining trait is not "manufacturing" but seasonality plus visual merchandising: a new line, several times a year, decided largely by looking at pictures of products next to each other.

The seasonal calendar — the spine of everything

A brand runs a GTM (go-to-market) calendar per season. Every conversation you have with a customer is anchored somewhere on it, so learn its shape first:

StageWhat happensWho leadsWhose system
Trend / conceptResearch, inspiration, mood boards, colour directionDesign, trendVibeIQ
Line planningHow many styles, in which categories, at what price points, hitting what marginMerchandising / planningVibeIQ
DesignSketches, colourways, materialsDesignVibeIQ
Line review / milestone meetingsAssortment is presented, edited live, cut or keptMerch + leadershipVibeIQ
Development & sourcingTech packs, BOMs, costing, vendors, samplesProduct dev, sourcingPLM
Sell-in / marketAssortment shown to wholesale buyers or internal channelsSalesVibeIQ
Production & launchPOs, inventory, ecommerce/retail launchSupply chain, ecomERP

VibeIQ lives in the top half — concept through line review, and again at sell-in. The bottom half (tech packs, BOM, costing, POs) belongs to PLM and ERP.

Key vocabulary — learn these cold

Customers use these constantly and will not stop to define them. Not knowing them is the fastest way to sound external in a room of merchants.

The style → colourway → SKU hierarchy is worth over-learning now. It is the mental model behind VibeIQ's property levels (Week 2), and property levels are the single concept most likely to be at the root of a "the data is wrong" escalation.

Concept 2 — The problem VibeIQ exists to solve

A consumer brand decides months in advance what products it will make for a season. Deciding which products, in which colors, at which price points, hitting which margin targets, for which regions — that's line planning. It happens before the expensive work of development and sourcing.

The problem: brands run separate systems for design, financial planning, product development, and selling, and many decisions about which products move forward still happen across disconnected documents and workflows. Teams lose the context behind key decisions once products enter development — nobody remembers why a product was chosen, dropped, or modified, and mistakes surface late when they're expensive to fix.

VibeIQ gives merchandising, design, planning, and product-development teams one live view of the product line connecting creative intent with commercial context, so they decide what moves forward before unnecessary cost, complexity, and margin risk build downstream. The claimed payoff: fewer planned SKUs, decisions visible months earlier, and thousands of manual hours eliminated.

Why customers buy it, in the order they'll usually say it:

  1. Cycle time — collapse the mood-board → spreadsheet → deck relay into one live workspace; line reviews stop needing a week of deck-building.
  2. One version of the truth in the decision phase — no more "which spreadsheet is current?"
  3. Better decisions — margin, cost and sell-through data sitting next to the image at the moment of the cut.
  4. Less manual work — no re-keying between Excel, PowerPoint and PLM.
  5. AI leverage — the platform is built so agents can act on the product data, rather than being bolted on.

The value you protect is earlier, better-informed product decisions with the reasoning preserved. When usage slips, the real questions are: "Are they deciding in VibeIQ, or drifting back to spreadsheets?" and "Is the context surviving the handoff downstream?"

Concept 3 — Where VibeIQ sits

Keystone

VibeIQ sits upstream of PLM, in the creative-to-commercial gap where products are still being decided rather than developed. It sits alongside PLM / ERP / PIM / DAM rather than replacing them, and integrates bi-directionally with all of them.

The PLM distinction, concrete:

  • PLM (e.g. Centric, PTC FlexPLM, Bamboo Rose) manages a product through development and production: tech packs, BOMs, sourcing, supplier collaboration, costing. The system of record for "how do we make and produce this."
  • VibeIQ manages the decision phase before all that: what should the line even be? The system of record for "what are we preparing to sell, and why."

Typical flow: decisions made in VibeIQ → approved products published to the PLM → PLM drives development/sourcing → ERP handles finance and production execution.

The full system landscape — what else is in the room

PLM and ERP are only the two loudest neighbours. You will be expected to draw all of this on a whiteboard:

What VibeIQ actually displaces

Not any system in that table — the tools it replaces are Miro, Excel and PowerPoint. That is VibeIQ's own framing: Board replaces Miro, Plan replaces Excel, Showcase replaces PowerPoint. The real incumbent in line planning is a spreadsheet with images pasted into it, emailed around.

This is usually your best answer to "we already have PLM, why do we need this?" — because it names the thing the customer is actually doing today, rather than arguing abstractly about categories. PLM is a system of record for how a product is made; it was never designed for a merchant and a designer to sit in a room and cut 400 styles down to 250 while looking at images. That decision-making layer is what VibeIQ owns.

The three end-user apps (system of engagement)

  • Board — visual workspace: trend research, mood boards, creative direction.
  • Plan — structured workspace: line planning and product definition (the grid/spreadsheet replacement, but image-first and live).
  • Showcase — presentation workspace: line reviews and stakeholder alignment.

The platform underneath (system of record + automation)

  • Product Data Platform — products, assortments, assets. (Week 2: the core data model.)
  • Agent layer — native agents, custom agent authoring, prompt templates.
  • Event & workflow engine — triggers and automation. (Week 6.)
  • Integration & app platform — connectors, APIs, MCP server, App Marketplace. (Weeks 4–6.)

"Upstream" sets the data direction, and it cuts both ways. VibeIQ is often where a product record is born (a concept, a placeholder, "we need 4 more knits at $79") before it is real enough for PLM. Later, PLM and ERP become authoritative for costs, materials and inventory, which flow back into VibeIQ to inform decisions. So most customers run a two-way integration with a clear attribute-level ownership map — and getting that map right is core TAM work. Nearly every escalation you'll see ("the data is wrong in Plan", "publish didn't reach PLM") is really a question about that map.

"Alongside" also constrains scope. If a customer asks VibeIQ to do BOM costing rollups or PO management, that's a "wrong system" conversation — not a feature request. Your hardest technical conversations are almost never about VibeIQ alone; they're about the seam between VibeIQ and the PLM/ERP: what data flows across it, which direction, when, and what breaks. That's why Weeks 4 & 5 are the technical heart of the program.

Concept 5 — Contrail vs. the marketing brand

The platform has two voices for the same thing:

  • Marketing / product voice (vibeiq.com): "AI-native product decision platform," role-based AI agents, concept generation, line-plan automation. Reflects a real 2026 repositioning; VibeIQ raised $22.5M (led by Volition Capital, Aug 2026) to push this direction.
  • Technical / engineering voice (docs.vibeiq.com): the platform is documented as Contrail — a configurable, extensible product workspace tool. SDK, CLI, and npm packages are all @contrail/*.

You'll code-switch constantly: AI-decision-platform language with a VP of Merchandising, Contrail/entity/workflow language with an integration engineer. When "Contrail" shows up in Slack, it just means "the platform internals."

Caution — don't overpromise AI

AI capabilities are heavily marketed but thinly documented publicly. Until you confirm internally what's GA vs. beta vs. roadmap, don't promise AI functionality you can't substantiate. Overpromising on AI is the fastest way for a new TAM to create a trust problem.

Module 2 · Week 2

Core data model

The keystone module. Almost every escalation you will ever handle resolves to something on this page: which entity is the value on, at which property level, and under which federatedId. Slow down here — Weeks 4, 5 and 9 assume it cold.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can (1) explain the Item / ProjectItem / AssortmentItem split and say what lives at each level, (2) choose the right property level for a given attribute and predict what happens on the next load, (3) explain what a federatedId is and diagnose the duplicate-records bug it causes, and (4) describe how Projects, Assortments and the Board/Plan/Showcase apps fit together.

Concept 1 — The three item layers

VibeIQ does not store "a product" once. There is one Item — and then two relationship entities that attach data to it in a particular context. That distinction is sharper than "three copies," and it is how the docs describe it:

  • "A ProjectItem ties an Item to a Project. While Item data is global, ProjectItem data is specific to a project (e.g., a season)."
  • "An Item's membership in an Assortment is represented by an AssortmentItem entity."

So an Item is the product; a ProjectItem is "this product, in this season"; an AssortmentItem is "this product, in this assortment." Each relationship carries its own data. This is the most important structural idea in the platform.

EntityScopeAnswersTypical data
ItemOrg-wide, season-independent"What is this product, ever?"Style number, name, category, base attributes, colour, size range
ProjectItemOne Project (≈ one season)"What is this product for SS26?"Season-specific attributes, that season's target price, lifecycle state
AssortmentItemOne Assortment"Is this product in this assortment, and on what terms?"Placement, assortment-specific overrides, ordering, channel/region terms

The nesting is strict and one-way, and the Loader enforces it — you cannot load the narrow thing without the broad thing:

  • ITEM alone loads Items only.
  • PROJECT_ITEM must be paired with ITEM — loads Item and ProjectItem in the same load.
  • ASSORTMENT must be paired with ITEM, and can also be paired with PROJECT_ITEM — loading all three instances together.

Why the design exists: a style lives across many seasons; its commercial framing differs each season; and the same season's product appears in many assortments on different terms. Flattening these into one record would either lose the history or duplicate the product.

How the levels interact on a write

When a property exists on more than one of these entities, the loader resolves it "based on the property's level designation (Family, Option, All, Override)." So the two ideas in this module — entity layer and property level — are not independent axes you consider one at a time. They compose on every write, which is why a value can land somewhere neither the customer nor the integration expected.

"The data is wrong in Plan" is nearly always a level question, not a corruption question. Ask: which entity is the user looking at, and which entity did the integration write to? A value written to the Item won't show up where a user expects if the app is displaying an AssortmentItem override — and vice versa. Train yourself to ask "at which level?" before you ask anything else.

Concept 2 — Property levels: Family, Option, All, Override

Property levels apply to entity types with hierarchical structure — Item and ProjectItem. They control how a value propagates through the Family → Option relationship. And they map straight onto Week 1's vocabulary:

The bridge from Week 1

Family ≈ style (the hoodie) · Option ≈ colourway (the hoodie in black). If you understood style → colourway → SKU last week, you already have the shape of this.

LevelWhat the docs say it doesUse it for
FamilyUpdates the single Family-level entity, and "those values will be copied down and persisted onto each of that Family's Options."True style-level facts: category, silhouette, description
Option"Will update the single Option-level entity and those values will be persisted only onto that Option entity."Genuinely per-colourway facts: colour code, option image
AllValues are "managed individually for every entity in a Family, regardless if it's a Family or Option level entity." Critically — "No copy down behavior exists for these properties."Values set explicitly at every node, with no inheritance at all
OverrideLike Family, the value "will copy down to all the Options' properties" — but "you can override the value on the Option level by specifying a different value."Defaults that usually hold but sometimes don't: price, margin target

In a CSV, column suffixes target the level: somePropertyFamily, somePropertyOption.

The three failure modes to recognise on sight

  1. Wrong level chosen at config time. Price modelled as Family instead of Override, so a single premium colourway can't carry its own price. Presents as "the system won't let us do this."
  2. Copy-down clobber. A Family property is reloaded and overwrites option-level edits users made by hand. Presents as "our data reverted overnight." This is the classic one.
  3. All vs Override confusion. The team expects inheritance, gets none, and every option comes up blank. Presents as "the load didn't work."

Property level is a configuration decision made early that is expensive to reverse, because changing it rewrites how existing values propagate. When you inherit an account, find out which properties are Family, which are Override, and which are All. That map predicts their future tickets better than any other single thing you could ask for.

Concept 3 — federatedId, and the duplicate-records bug

Definition

A federatedId is "a value you provide which uniquely identifies the entity within an org." It drives upsert: "If an entity with that federatedId does not exist in VibeIQ, it will be created. If it does exist, it will be updated."

For Custom Entities, "if no property is specified as the federatedId, the out of the box name property will be used."

The trap, stated plainly in the docs

Entities created through the core applications — i.e. by users clicking around in the UI — do not have a federatedId by default. If you later want the Loader to update those records, someone has to assign one first.

This is the root cause of the most common integration bug in the platform: duplicates. A customer pilots by hand-creating products in the UI, then wires up a Loader feed keyed on style number. Every record loads as new, because the hand-made ones had no federatedId to match against. Nothing errors. They now have two of everything, and they discover it in a line review.

Ask about federatedId strategy before the first production load. And ask a second question: what is the federatedId derived from? If it's a mutable business value — a style number merchants renumber mid-season — the upsert key changes underneath you and you get orphans. Stable meaningless keys beat meaningful ones.

Concept 4 — Projects, Assortments and their owners

Project — the season-scale container. The Project Configurator creates "Projects with nested folders, plans, assortments, showcases, and boards," with assortments possible at both project root and folder level.

Assortment — a curated selection of products for a purpose: a channel, region, customer, or store cluster. One line → many assortments, exactly as in Week 1.

An assortment "can be created on its own or it can have an associated plan, board, or showcase":

Load typeCreates
ASSORTMENTAn assortment on its own
PLANA Plan and its assortment
BOARDA Board and its assortment
SHOWCASEA Showcase and its assortment

Assortment membership is replaced, not merged, by default

A full assortment load has explicit semantics: CSV items not in the assortment are added; items in both are updated if changed; matching items with no changes are unchanged; and "assortment items not in CSV → deleted." That last clause is the whole risk — membership is defined by the file, so an incomplete file is a deletion instruction. Week 4 covers the flag that changes this (partialAssortmentUpdate); carry the semantics from here.

The link back to Week 1

Board, Plan and Showcase are each backed by an assortment. They are three views onto the same kind of underlying object — which is exactly why a product can travel from mood board to line plan to line review without being re-created. The apps are the surface; the assortment is the substance.

Concept 5 — Where this is heading: the publish lifecycle

A preview of Week 3, included here because it is what the data model is for. When a user publishes a Plan, VibeIQ persists the Plan rows down to the underlying Assortment and creates an AssortmentPublishChange — "a snapshot of those changes" for downstream systems. It carries:

  • assortmentBaselineDownloadLink — an S3 URL for "the Assortment's complete state at publish time"
  • deleteDataDownloadLink — an S3 URL with "only Items that were dropped or deleted in this specific publish"
  • detail — arrays of adds, updates, deletes, familyItemsRemoved

Two facts to memorise now — both cause production incidents

  • Deletes do not accumulate. "Delete data does NOT accumulate across publishes. Each AssortmentPublishChange only contains deletes from that individual publish." Miss one publish and those deletes are permanently missed — unless you re-baseline.
  • S3 links expire after 24 hours. Re-fetching the change object refreshes them. A consumer that stores the URL breaks; one that stores the change ID can always recover.

Module 3 · Week 3

End-user apps & the publish lifecycle

What your users actually touch, and what happens the moment one of them hits publish. This is the module that connects Week 2's data model to Weeks 4 and 5's integrations — the publish seam is where "it works in VibeIQ but not in PLM" lives.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can (1) explain what each of Board, Plan and Showcase is for and what it replaced, (2) walk the publish lifecycle end to end and name what happens at each step, (3) describe an AssortmentPublishChange field by field and say what a consumer should do with each, and (4) localise a "published but didn't arrive" failure to one of four checkpoints before theorising about causes.

Concept 1 — The three apps

Each app replaces a specific incumbent tool — this is VibeIQ's own framing, from Week 1 — and, from Week 2, each is backed by an Assortment.

AppVibeIQ's descriptionReplaces
Board"The visual workspace for trend research, mood boards, and creative direction"Miro
Plan"The structured workspace for line planning and product definition"Excel
Showcase"The presentation workspace for line reviews and stakeholder alignment"PowerPoint

The technically important detail in each:

  • Board connects unstructured creative work directly to product data, so agents can interpret and act on creative intent rather than on a picture of it.
  • Plan — "each row represents an actual product record." This is the whole difference from a spreadsheet: the grid is a view onto entities, not a document. Editing a row edits a ProjectItem or AssortmentItem.
  • Showcase "remains live and current, with feedback flowing back into the product record." A deck that is a rendering of live data, not an export of it.

When a customer mentions they "exported to PowerPoint for the line review," treat it as an adoption failure with a technical smell underneath — usually Showcase not being trusted, or the product data being too incomplete to present without cleanup. Chase the cause, not the behaviour. The same logic applies to any export-to-spreadsheet habit that survives go-live.

Concept 2 — The platform underneath

  • Product Data Platform — products, assortments, assets (Week 2's territory).

Concept 3 — The publish lifecycle, end to end

  1. Users modify Items — products, materials — inside a Plan.
  2. Users adopt images, colours and attributes onto those Items.
  3. A user triggers publish.
  4. The system persists the Plan rows down to the underlying Assortment.
  5. An AssortmentPublishChange is created — "a snapshot of those changes."
  6. External systems retrieve and process it.

The lifecycle can be bypassed entirely

A Loader configuration can set shouldSkipAssortmentPublish: true, which writes assortment item updates straight to the database — "bypassing the publish workflow… skips change history, summary recalculation, composite assortment updates, and the assortment|publish event." Everything on this page then does not happen: no AssortmentPublishChange, no event, nothing for a consumer to receive. Week 4 covers it in full; carry the fact from here, because "the data is in VibeIQ but PLM never heard" is a Module 3 symptom with a Module 4 cause.

The step everyone skips

Step 4. Publish is not "send to PLM." Publish is first an internal commit from the Plan down to the Assortment. The downstream integration is a consumer of the record that commit produces. So a publish can succeed while the downstream sync fails — and those are two different investigations, with different owners.

Concept 4 — Anatomy of an AssortmentPublishChange

FieldContentsWhat a consumer does with it
assortmentBaselineDownloadLinkS3 URL — "the Assortment's complete state at publish time"Recovery and reconciliation. The answer to "what is true now?"
deleteDataDownloadLinkS3 URL — "only Items that were dropped or deleted in this specific publish"Apply removals. The field most often ignored, and the one that causes ghost products downstream.
detailadds, updates, deletes, familyItemsRemovedEfficient incremental sync. The answer to "what changed this time?"

Most real consumers need both: detail for day-to-day efficiency, baseline for recovery when detail has been missed.

The two rules that cause production incidents

  • Deletes do not accumulate. "Delete data does NOT accumulate across publishes. Each AssortmentPublishChange only contains deletes from that individual publish." A consumer that misses publish #15 has permanently missed those deletes. Nothing later will replay them. The only cure is re-baselining.
  • S3 links expire after 24 hours. Re-fetching the change object refreshes them. Store the change ID, never the URL — a consumer that persists the URL breaks after a day; one that persists the ID can always recover.

Non-accumulating deletes make missed publishes silently unrecoverable. Nothing errors; the downstream system simply keeps products the brand has already cut. It surfaces weeks later as "PLM has styles we dropped" — usually in front of someone senior. On any account with a publish integration, ask early: what happens when your consumer is down for a day, and who notices?

Module 4 · Week 4

Data in: the Loader framework

With Week 5, the technical heart of the program. The Loader is where customer data becomes VibeIQ entities — and where a large share of your escalations will originate. The goal this week is to read a failed load the way an engineer reads a stack trace: know the phase, know the artefact, know the field.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can (1) name the five phases and six preprocessing steps and say what each produces, (2) read and write a loader configuration and predict its effect on a given CSV, (3) interpret a failed load from failureArea, issueType and the per-step artefacts, (4) explain the four configuration flags that change what a load fundamentally does — including one that silently bypasses the publish lifecycle — and (5) predict which workflows a load will set off, and when it won't.

Concept 1 — A load is an entity, not a command

A LoaderProcess is an asynchronous entity holding the metadata of an import: execution status, the configuration parameters used, downloadable process logs, and operational metadata. Creating one via API, SDK or CLI enqueues it.

Two fields make it a diagnostic instrument rather than a receipt:

  • failureArea — where it broke: NONE, PARSING_CSV, VALIDATING_DATA, or TRANSFORMING_DATA.
  • preprocessingStepResults — an array of file IDs, one per step, each holding the data as it stood after that step.

Ask for the process ID the way you would ask for a request ID, then read failureArea before anything else. It localises the failure to one of four segments in a single field — the cheapest diagnostic move available anywhere in the platform. When a customer says a load behaved differently than last week, compare the LoaderProcess records rather than taking anyone's word for it; the configuration used is recorded on each one.

Concept 2 — The five phases

  1. CSV parsing — "The CSV file is downloaded and parsed... Column headers from the first row are used as property keys." Failures here set PARSING_CSV.
  2. Validation — data checked against entity-type rules, before transformations. Failures set VALIDATING_DATA.
  3. Preprocessing — the six-step transformation sequence.
  4. Entity construction — rows become entity objects matching the schema, correctly typed.
  5. API upsert — create or update, matched by federatedId. Failures in 3–5 set TRANSFORMING_DATA.

Concept 3 — Validation: fail-fast, and no partial loads

The rule that shapes everything downstream

"If validation produces any errors, the load stops immediately with a VALIDATING_DATA failure area." Errors halt the job; warnings let it continue. No partial loads occur — you never get half a file applied. Most people assume the opposite, and the assumption changes how they plan a recovery.

Validation checks four categories — configuration completeness, required field availability, type path validity, and data integrity. Every load, regardless of entity type, requires a non-empty config, a loadType with at least one value, and both federatedId and name reachable "from csv headers, federatedMappings, or conditionalColumns."

The error taxonomy

A row-level issue looks like this:

{
  "rowNumber": 5,

    {
      "columnName": "itemFamilyFederatedId",

A file- or config-level issue carries no row number:

  "message": "typePath must be set to 'color' via csv data, conditionalColumn, or federatedMappings.",
  "issueType": "CONFIG"
}

Error messages worth recognising on sight: "itemFamilyFederatedId cannot be empty" (per-row) and "The following families have conflicting federated IDs: …" (job-level — the same family arriving with two different IDs).

Warnings that do not stop a load come from steps 4 and 6 (unrecognised columns, archived properties), option sets (values not matching defined options), size ranges (missing templates, invalid sizes), and object references (federated IDs with no matching entity). These accumulate in the step result files.

Fail-fast is good news you should say out loud. "Nothing was partially applied — the file was rejected as a whole" is reassuring, and it means the fix is fix-and-rerun rather than a reconciliation exercise. The corollary matters too: a load that reports completed genuinely applied everything it accepted, so if data looks wrong afterwards, the problem is in transformation or configuration — not in a half-finished job.

Concept 4 — The six preprocessing steps

#StepWhat it does
1Boolean conversionConverts "true"/"false" strings to real booleans — CSV parsers only ever produce strings.
2Federated mappingsFor each { targetProperty: sourceColumn }: if the source column exists, its value is copied to the target; if it doesn't, the literal value is set on all rows. Original columns are preserved.
3Remove unwanted propertiesDeletes the propertiesToRemove columns from every row.
4Type transformations (no warnings)Label-to-slug for headers, and display-to-value for Single/Multi Select backed by option sets.
5Conditional columnsApplies conditionalColumns to create or overwrite values, with {columnName} interpolation and JavaScript evaluation.
6Type transformations (with warnings)Repeats step 4, this time recording warnings for unmatched columns, disabled properties and invalid option values.

Why steps 4 and 6 are the same thing twice

Step 4 runs the type transformations silently, so conditional columns in step 5 can operate on already-normalised values. Step 6 re-runs them and reports. The consequence: a value can be silently altered in step 4 and never generate a warning if step 5 subsequently overwrites the column. When a value doesn't match the CSV and no warning exists, suspect the 4/5/6 interaction and read the artefacts rather than reasoning about it.

The artefacts are the whole game

Each step produces rows, errors and warnings, saved as loader-process-step-<Step Name>.json and listed in the preprocessingStepResults array on the LoaderProcess. You can see the data after every single step — diff consecutive files and the step that broke a value is unambiguous. Reach them via the Admin Console (attached files on the LoaderProcess), or by iterating preprocessingStepResults from the API or SDK. Most people argue about loads; you can just look.

interface ColumnDefinition {
  toProperty: string;          // target column (overwrites if it exists)

  conditions?: ConditionalValues[];   // first match wins

Three shapes you'll meet. A static value:

Concept 7 — The four flags that change what a load is

Most configuration fields shape how data is mapped. These four change the nature of the operation, and three of them do damage quietly.

1 · shouldSkipAssortmentPublish — the one that breaks integrations invisibly

When true, "assortment item updates are written directly to the database, bypassing the publish workflow. This skips change history, summary recalculation, composite assortment updates, and the assortment|publish event."

Read that list again. No AssortmentPublishChange. No change history. No assortment|publish event — so every downstream consumer, whether event-driven or polling change history, never learns the data moved. The load succeeds. VibeIQ is correct. PLM is stale, indefinitely, with nothing anywhere reporting a problem.

The flag has a legitimate purpose: the docs recommend it for a "high volume of changes" where "full history is not required" — a migration or backfill where you genuinely don't want a publish per row. The failure is that it gets set once "just for the migration" and stays in a reusable configuration forever. Put it on your pre-load checklist as a question with two parts: is this flag set, and is that still deliberate? Then: if it is set, who re-syncs the downstream system, and when?

2 · partialAssortmentUpdate — a default that deletes

Defaults to false. When false, an assortment load replaces the assortment's contents; when true, items are added and dropped rather than replaced.

So sending a partial file — say, only the 40 styles that changed — against the default configuration does not update 40 items. It leaves the assortment containing only those 40. The load reports success.

The full-load semantics, precisely: CSV items not in the assortment are added; items in both are updated if changed; unchanged matches are left unchanged; and "assortment items not in CSV → deleted."

Partial updates use two boolean columns: addField marks rows to add or update, dropField (or top-level assortmentItemDropField) marks rows to remove. One rule worth knowing: "items marked for add that already exist in the assortment are treated as updates instead," so an add on an existing item is safe rather than duplicating.

Style Name,addItem,dropItem,gender
Classic Tee,false,true,womens     → removed from the womens assortment
V-Neck Tee,true,false,womens      → added, or updated if already present
                                  → everything else in the assortment is untouched

3 · assortmentSplit — one file, many assortments

Routes rows to different assortments based on a column value — fieldToSplitOn plus a values array mapping each value to an assortmentId or assortmentIdentifier. "The Loader groups rows by the value in the fieldToSplitOn column. Each group is matched to an assortment from the values array." One load can therefore affect several assortments, and so trigger several downstream publishes.

The catch: "rows with fieldToSplitOn values not matching any entry are skipped." Not errored — skipped. A new region code, a typo, a trailing space, and those rows quietly never load while the job reports success. Any change to the vocabulary of that column is a silent data-loss event, so the values array needs to be reviewed whenever the source system gains a new category.

4 · workspaceIdentifier — and a genuine UI gap

Required whenever loadType includes PROJECT_ITEM, "even when also loading assortments." It matches a project's identifier exactly.

Two things to know. First, vocabulary: a "project" in the UI is a workspace with workspaceType: 'PROJECT' in the API — worth recognising when reading API responses or error messages. Second, the trap: the identifier "is not editable in the Hub UI; it must be set via direct PUT request to the workspace entity." So if a project's identifier is wrong or missing, an admin cannot fix it by clicking — someone has to make an API call.

Concept 8 — federatedId, with the mechanics

There is no "which column is my ID?" setting

"There is no configuration to say 'this column is my Federated ID'." The only exception is Custom Entity, and if none is specified there, "the out of the box name property will be used." For everything else the value must arrive under the expected property name — which in practice means federatedMappings or a conditionalColumn puts it there.

The stability rule, verbatim: "It is critical to ensure that federatedId values in your load files do not change between loads. Doing so will risk the Loader creating duplicate data."

And the same bug from another angle: entities created in the core apps have no federatedId. To update that data by Loader, values must be assigned manually through the API — the platform "doesn't synchronize property values with federatedId internally."

Three facts, one predictable story: the source system renumbers a style, the federatedId changes, the next load creates a second entity, and neither the load nor the app reports anything wrong. Note that validation does catch duplicates within a single file (DUPLICATE_FEDERATED_ID, and "conflicting federated IDs" for families) — but nothing catches a value that differs from last week's file. Your prevention question stays one sentence: "What generates this value, and what could ever cause it to change?" — and now you know to check conditionalColumns for the answer as well as the source system.

Concept 11 — What a load does to your workflows

A load is an event storm — usually

Phase 5 upserts entities, so a load emits ordinary system events: item|create for new rows, item|update for existing ones, and likewise for project-item and assortment-item. Every event workflow bound to those triggers fires, once per row. A 20,000-row load is 20,000 trigger evaluations.

…except when it deliberately isn't

With shouldSkipAssortmentPublish: true, the assortment|publish event is not emitted. So the storm is selective: entity-level workflows still fire, but anything hanging off publish — including the outbound integration workflow from Week 5 — stays silent. This is why "we loaded it and PLM never got it" can be true with no failure anywhere. Check the flag before you check the consumer.

Three consequences to hold together:

  1. Create vs. update changes behaviour. From Week 6, {oldData} is absent on creates, so a conditional like {newData.status} !== {oldData.status} evaluates differently for new rows than existing ones in the very same load. A load can therefore notify for part of a file and not the rest — which looks like a bug and is not.
  2. Concurrency ceilings are shared. A bulk load can saturate the org's 60-process budget, starving unrelated automation. "Our other integration got slow on Tuesday" is often "someone ran a big load on Tuesday."
  3. Races become likely precisely when load is heaviest. A workflow with a high parallelWorkerCount and no message grouping may be fine daily and corrupt data during a bulk load, because that is when colliding events actually overlap.

Before any large or first-time load, ask: which workflows are bound to these entity types, what do they do on create, is anything grouped, and is publish being skipped? If the answer involves notifications or outbound sync, plan for it — deactivate, narrow the conditional, or schedule deliberately. "The load worked but it emailed 3,000 people" and "the load worked but nothing reached PLM" are both avoidable, and avoiding them is exactly what a TAM is valued for.

Data out: integration patterns

The other half of the technical heart. Week 4 was data arriving; this is data leaving, and it is the seam customers actually break. By the end you should be able to sit in an architecture review, read someone's integration design, and name what will fail in production before it does.

Learning objectives

Concept 2 — Authentication and the API surface

Concept 4 — The polling path, and the seam

Polling hits /api/change-history "for changes to entities within a specified time range." The consumer stores a last-sync timestamp, builds a date-range criterion from it, polls, processes, and stores the new timestamp. Filtering is temporal — createdOn accepts a BETWEEN 2024-01-01 and 2024-12-31 form — and "this approach gives you full control over when and how frequently you retrieve changes."

Temporal filtering is not a cursor, and the difference is the bug

A cursor is a position in an ordered sequence: resume from it and you cannot miss or repeat. A timestamp is a guess about a position, and three things break it:

  • Boundary exclusivity. Poll [lastSync, now] and records at exactly lastSync are processed twice; poll (lastSync, now] and any record written in the same instant as your cutoff is lost forever.
  • In-flight writes. A record whose createdOn falls just before now but which commits just after your query runs is never returned by this poll, and is before the cutoff on the next one. It vanishes silently.
  • Clock skew. The consumer's clock and the server's clock are not the same clock. A consumer that computes now locally can skip a window.

The guards, in order of importance:

  1. Overlap the window. Poll from lastSync − safetyMargin (a minute or two), not from lastSync. Deliberately re-fetch a little.
  2. Make processing idempotent. Overlap only helps if reprocessing the same record is harmless. Key on entity ID plus a change identifier and no-op on repeats.
  3. Advance the timestamp from the data, not the clock. Use the maximum createdOn actually returned, not the local time at which you polled.
  4. Never advance on partial failure. If processing threw halfway, the timestamp must not move.

The symptom this produces is the most frustrating you will meet: occasional missing records, no errors anywhere, and no reproducibility. Both systems look healthy. When a customer reports "we're missing maybe one in a few hundred, we can't work out which," stop looking at the data and go read how their polling window is computed. Almost every time, it is exclusive boundaries or a locally-computed now.

Customers who do this find problems in days; customers who don't find them in line reviews. Recommending it is cheap advice that makes you look prescient about a year later.

Concept 6 — Webhook security, in both directions

The two directions have genuinely different postures, and conflating them is how you give a security team a wrong answer.

EndpointPOST /api/trigger-webhooks/{ID}The customer's own URL

What to actually say to a security team

Outbound is defensible: HMAC signing is available, and you should recommend it over header keys or IP allowlists. Inbound is weaker — the trigger URL contains the webhook ID and functions as a bearer credential, and the documented verification is an optional shared-secret header rather than a signature over the request body. Treat trigger URLs as secrets, don't put them anywhere loggable, and confirm internally what is actually enforced server-side before you make a claim. Note also that outbound configuration goes through VibeIQ support — so it is a lead-time item in any implementation plan, not a self-serve toggle.

Concept 7 — Attribute ownership, the artefact that prevents the arguments

Two-way integration means every attribute needs an owner. Without an explicit map, both systems write the same field and the last writer wins — intermittently, and differently each time.

The map you should insist exists, per attribute: which system is authoritative · which direction it flows · at which entity layer it lives (Item / ProjectItem / AssortmentItem) · at which property level (Family / Option / All / Override) · what happens on conflict.

This single artefact resolves more disputes than any diagnostic skill. "PLM overwrote our margin target" is not a bug report — it is the absence of this map. Build it during implementation, keep it current, and bring it to every architecture review. Weeks 2, 4 and 5 exist so that you can fill it in credibly, which is why the columns name the layer and the property level rather than just the field.

Module 6 · Week 6

Event Workflows

The deepest technical module in the program, and the one closest to your day job. Workflows are "the main engine for reacting to system events and running apps across the VibeIQ infrastructure" — when a customer says "the automation didn't run," this is the entire surface you have to reason about.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can (1) read a workflow definition in app.yml and predict exactly what it will do, (2) name all six trigger categories and pick the right one for a requirement, (3) explain path evaluation and the first-match-wins rule that causes silent misbehaviour, (4) diagnose a concurrency race and prescribe the right message-grouping fix, and (5) work the "why didn't it fire?" ladder from the Admin Console down to taskOutputs.

Concept 1 — Anatomy

"Workflows are composed of triggers, conditionals, and actions." A trigger says when, conditionals say which branch, actions say what happens. Workflows are declared in an app's app.yml under a top-level workflows: key.

workflows:

    identifier: 'syncToProduction'
    name: 'Sync Items to Production'
    triggerKey: item|update
    isActive: true
    public: true
    parallelWorkerCount: 5
    workflowDefinition:
      paths:
        - conditional: 'true'
          actionDefinitionConfigs:
            - action: syncItem
              name: Sync Item

Concept 2 — The six trigger categories

1. System events — entity CRUD broadcast when data changes, in the form {entityType}|{action}:

If a customer wants automation on "any change to the assortment," the honest answer is that they must hang it off assortment-item events or off publish, and those have very different timing. This is a requirements conversation, not a config task, and catching it early saves a rebuild.

2. Manual — triggerKey: manual. Not tied to an event; used for batch loads, archival jobs and on-demand tasks. Also your safest way to test an action in isolation.

3. Custom app events — react to another app's domain events:

triggerKey: '@{publisherOrgSlug}/{appName}:{EventName}'
# e.g. '@vibeiq/image-loader:ImageAssignmentComplete'

The {EventName} part comes from the publishing app's eventWorkflowTriggerKeyMapping template. This is how apps chain — and how a break in app A silently stops automation in app B.

4. Declared custom trigger keys — domain-specific triggers an app declares:

triggers:
  CopyDownToLinkedItems:
    key: item|linked-copy-down
    name: Trigger copy down to linked item copies
    description: Triggers a workflow to copy down item updates to linked item copies.

Fired programmatically by creating an external-event entity:

await new Entities().create({
  entityName: 'external-event',
  object: {
    details: { eventType: 'item' },
    actionType: 'linked-copy-down',
    // ...payload fields the conditionals and actions will read
  },
});

5. Scheduled — crons declarations using standard CRON syntax.

6. Webhook — external HTTP callbacks, covered in Concept 7.

Concept 3 — Paths, and the rule that bites

workflowDefinition.paths is an ordered array. Each path pairs a conditional with a list of actionDefinitionConfigs.

First match wins

"Only the first path whose conditional evaluates to true is executed; the remaining paths are skipped." Paths are an if / else-if chain, not a set of independent rules. Actions within a path run sequentially.

paths:
  - conditional: '{newData.status} === "active"'
    actionDefinitionConfigs:
      - action: notifySlack
        name: Notify Slack (Active)
      - action: logItem
        name: Log Item
  - conditional: 'true'          # catch-all — only reached if the above was false
    actionDefinitionConfigs:
      - action: archiveItem
        name: Archive Item

The classic authoring bug

Someone writes several paths intending "run all that apply," and puts a broad or true conditional near the top. Everything below it becomes dead code. Nothing errors, the process completes successfully, and the customer reports "only some of the automation runs." Whenever you review a workflow, read the paths top-down and find the first one that can always be true — everything after it is unreachable.

Concept 4 — Conditional expressions

Conditionals are JavaScript expressions evaluated against the event payload, with interpolations wrapped in { ... }. Available inputs:

InputWhat it holds
{newData.<slug>}Entity state after the change
{oldData.<slug>}State before the change — absent on creates
{propertyDiffs.<slug>.<propertyName|oldValue|newValue>}Map of changed-property metadata
{changeObjects}Array of { id, changes } for bulk-change workflows
 
conditional: 'true'                                          # always run
conditional: '{newData.roles}.includes("family")'            # family-level only
conditional: '{propertyDiffs.status.propertyName} != null'   # did status change at all?
conditional: '{newData.status} !== {oldData.status}'         # old vs new
conditional: '{newData.status} === "approved" && {newData.roles}.includes("family")'

For bulk propagation, changeObjects lets one conditional test many entities at once:

conditional: >-
  {changeObjects}.some(obj =>
    ["sizeGroup","merchDivisionCode","brand","primaryViewableId"].some(prop =>
      Object.keys(obj.changes).includes(prop)))

{oldData} being absent on creates is a genuine trap. A conditional like {newData.status} !== {oldData.status} behaves differently on a create than an update — and a bulk load creates thousands of entities at once. "The workflow fired on every row of the initial load and spammed everyone" is a real incident, and this is usually why. When reviewing, always ask what the conditional does on a create.

Concept 5 — Actions and per-workflow config

Task execution is strictly serial, and fails closed

"For each time a workflow is triggered, a process is created. Each process contains one or more tasks." And crucially: "Only one task runs at a time in a process, and tasks always run in order. If one task fails, none of the subsequent tasks are run." So a three-action path that fails on action one leaves actions two and three unexecuted — not failed, not retried, simply never run. Order your actions so the riskiest one is last, and never assume a later action ran because the process exists.

Each entry in actionDefinitionConfigs needs action (a key matching the app's actions section) and name (the label shown in the Admin UI), plus optional config. Actions exposing actionConfiguration can be reused with different parameters per workflow:

actions:
  syncItem:
    name: 'Sync Item'
    async: true
    actionConfiguration:
      endpoint:
        label: 'Target API Endpoint'
        propertyType: 'string'

# ...then, per workflow:
    - action: syncItem
      name: Sync Item
      config:
        endpoint: 'https://prod.example.com/items'

Config values are "merged with runtime context and passed to the action's execute function via the config argument." One action, many environments — which is exactly how a staging endpoint ends up wired into a production workflow. Check config values during any environment-promotion review.

Access grants

Actions can only touch entities the app has been granted:

accessGrants:
  - entityType: item
    accessLevel: write        # get, query, create, update, delete
  - entityType: project-item
    accessLevel: read         # get, query

A workflow that fires, runs, and "does nothing" is often a missing or read-only access grant.

Concept 6 — Concurrency and message grouping

This is the part that produces the hardest bugs you will see, because they are intermittent and load-dependent.

Two serialization strategies:

Static serialization — every event for this workflow runs strictly one at a time:

copyDownToLinkedItemsWorkflow:
  triggerKey: item|linked-copy-down
  messageGroupId: copyDownToLinkedItems
  parallelWorkerCount: 1

Dynamic grouping — events sharing a key serialize; different keys run in parallel:

propagateRegionalForecasts:
  triggerKey: project-item|update
  parallelWorkerCount: 30
  messageGroup: propagateRegionalForecasts
  dynamicMessageGroupId: 'propagateRegionalForecasts-{newData.itemFamilyId}'

The idea worth internalising

Dynamic grouping lets you serialize only where it matters. Keying on itemFamilyId means two events touching the same family queue behind each other — no lost update — while events on different families run concurrently up to parallelWorkerCount. You get correctness where entities collide and throughput everywhere else. Static messageGroupId with parallelWorkerCount: 1 is the safe, slow hammer; dynamic grouping is the right tool once volume matters.

LimitDefault
parallelWorkerCount range1–30, default 5
Concurrent processes per event workflow25
Concurrent processes per organization60

The per-workflow and per-org ceilings are adjustable via the Customer Success Manager. Separately, "workflows can be configured to have limits on their run per minute" — a rate limit distinct from the concurrency ceiling, and the right lever when a workflow is correct but too chatty for a downstream system to absorb.

Disable, don't delete

"Workflows can be disabled without deleting them. This is useful when you wish to test out various workflows for a limited period of time, and keep them for historical record without changing their trigger." Deletion is different in kind: "Once deleted, the process list, task list, and logs for a workflow is lost." So deleting a workflow destroys its entire evidence trail instantly — not after 90 days, immediately. Before any cleanup of "old workflows," ask whether anyone might need to explain something that happened last month.

Two things to carry into customer conversations. First, the per-org ceiling of 60 is shared — one chatty workflow during a bulk load can starve every other workflow in the org, and the symptom is "unrelated automation got slow today." Second, raising parallelWorkerCount to fix slowness without a message group is how you convert a performance complaint into a data-corruption incident. Throughput and correctness are the same dial here; never turn it without asking what collides.

Concept 7 — Webhooks

Create one via CLI (contrail webhooks create -j) or by POSTing to https://api.vibeiq.com/prod/api/webhooks. The response carries an id you need later. Authentication uses two headers:

  • X-Api-Key — your API key
  • X-Api-Org — your organization key

Link a webhook to a workflow by setting the trigger to "webhookId:WEBHOOK_ID". Multiple workflows can hang off one webhook. To fire it:

curl --request POST \
  --url https://api.vibeiq.com/prod/api/trigger-webhooks/4X0bETHdBKw87l6h \
  --header 'X-Verify-Signature: my-secret-key'

The workflow receives the request as an event shaped:

{
  "body":    { /* your POST data */ },
  "headers": { /* request headers */ }
}

The trigger endpoint returns an array of the workflow processes it created, with statuses, IDs and initial event data — useful for confirming a webhook actually started something.

Security posture — flag this one

This is the inbound direction (external system → VibeIQ), and its posture is the weaker of the two. X-Verify-Signature is documented as optional and, in the example, is a shared secret compared as a header value — not an HMAC over the request body — while a webhook ID in a URL is effectively a bearer credential. The outbound direction (VibeIQ → the customer's endpoint, covered in Week 5) does support HMAC signing. Keep the two apart when a security team asks, treat trigger URLs as secrets, and confirm internally what inbound verification is actually enforced.

Concept 8 — From app to running workflow

  1. Workflows are declared in app.yml.
  2. contrail app publish turns them into Workflow Template Definitions attached to that app version.
  3. An installing org runs contrail workflow-template-definitions install to instantiate the templates as live workflows, preserving config values end to end.

Workflows are versioned artefacts of an app, not settings someone edits in a console. So "can you just tweak that automation?" is a release question: change the app, publish a version, install it. That distinction is the config-vs-customization boundary (Week 7) showing up in practice, and it sets a customer's expectations about turnaround.

Concept 9 — Observability, and the "why didn't it fire?" ladder

The diagnostic ladder — work it in order

  1. Is there a process at all? Filter the Process List on the entity. No process means the trigger never matched — go to 2. A process means it fired — jump to 5.
  2. Is isActive true, and is the workflow actually installed in this org from the app version they think?
  3. Is the triggerKey the right event? The classic miss: expecting assortment|update, which does not exist.
  4. Did the change actually emit that event? A bulk load creating entities emits create, not update.
  5. Which path ran? Read triggerEvent, then evaluate the conditionals top-down yourself. Remember first-match-wins and that {oldData} is absent on creates.
  6. Did the actions succeed? Read taskOutputs per task. A COMPLETE process with a no-op action usually means a missing access grant or a config pointing somewhere harmless.
  7. Is it a timing problem? Intermittent, load-correlated, "sometimes the value is stale" — suspect concurrency and message grouping, not logic.

Module 7 · Week 7

Apps, Extensions & the config/customization boundary

How far you can go without code, where the line actually is, and what crossing it costs in money, time and people. This is the judgement call you'll be asked to make on nearly every account — usually phrased as "can the platform just do X?"

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can (1) place any customer request on the configuration→customization spectrum and justify it, (2) write and debug formulas and rule sets, including their silent failure modes, (3) explain how configuration moves between orgs and what that means for environment strategy, (4) describe app identity, ownership and versioning — and the decisions at app creation that can never be undone, and (5) say what an extension is, what it costs to run, and when it's the wrong answer.

Concept 1 — The spectrum

"Configuration vs. customization" is a false binary. It's a ladder, and each rung costs more to build, more to change, and involves more people.

Your default question for any "can it do X?" is "what is the lowest rung that solves this?" Customers and implementation partners both drift upward — engineers reach for code because code is what they do. Every rung you avoid climbing is change latency the customer keeps and support surface you don't inherit. Equally, forcing a Level 1 answer onto a Level 2 problem produces a fragile pile of formulas nobody can maintain. The skill is naming the right rung, not the lowest one.

Concept 2 — Formulas

Formulas assign property values programmatically from entity state. Inputs vary by entity type:

EntityAvailable inputs
All entitiesobj (the entity's properties), context.previousObj (pre-update state)
Itemsplus obj.roles — the array distinguishing family from option
Plan placeholdersobj.itemFamily, obj.itemOption, obj.itemType
Project itemsobj.item, context.project
Assortment itemsobj.item (option-preferred, falling back to family)

Return types must match the property: "If the Property Type is \"number\", then the formula should return a numeric value," and dates must be ISO strings via toISOstring().

Evaluation timing

  • Run on create and update for all entities.
  • ProjectItem formulas re-run on Item updates.
  • Plan placeholder formulas run on load, on placeholder updates, and on any row cell modification.
  • Plan publish triggers all Item, ProjectItem and AssortmentItem formulas.

Three silent failure modes — memorise these

  1. Misspelled slugs return undefined, silently. The docs are explicit: "Verify every slug against the live Type before saving a formula." No error, no warning — just an empty or wrong value. This is the single most common formula bug, and it looks exactly like a data problem.
  2. "Updates to Reference Data does not cause Formulas to re-run." So a formula that derives from reference data produces a value that is correct when written and quietly stale forever after. The customer changes a lookup, nothing recalculates, and they discover it a season later.
  3. "On the Frontend, only Formulas in Plans are run." Plus only one level of object hydration — you cannot reach nested references beyond immediate properties. A formula that works in one surface may not evaluate in another.

Concept 3 — Rule Sets

Rule Sets "control whether a property is editable, required, what option values are allowed in a dropdown, and what min/max range is permitted on a number — based on the current state of the entity."

A rule set is JSON with a name (used by CLI and Configuration Copy), an identifier (immutable, unique per org), and a ruleSet array of conditional blocks. Each block has criteria (when it applies) and rules (what it constrains). A rule targets a property by slug and can set:

FieldEffect
editablefalse locks the property; true is the default
requiredBlocks save when empty
optionSetValuesAllow-list of permitted values
minValue / maxValueNumeric boundaries
validationFunctionOverrides default validation

"ALL" applies a rule to every property; excludedSlugs carves out exceptions. Filter conditions include equals, not_equal_to, starts_with, contains, less_than, greater_than, is_empty, is_not_empty, is_any_of — validity depending on property type.

Evaluation semantics — get these exactly right

  • The outer array is OR — each block tests independently.
  • Inner criteria are AND — all conditions in a block must match.
  • "Editability is 'locked wins'" — if any matching rule says editable: false, the property is locked. You cannot re-open it with a later rule.
  • Validation constraints accumulate across all matching rules.

Rule Sets are a UX guardrail, not a data-integrity guarantee

The docs state rule sets "evaluate client-side, affecting editor experience for all users." That strongly implies the Loader and the API are not bound by them — a load can write a value a rule set forbids in the UI. Treat this as the working assumption, because the alternative (assuming enforcement) is the dangerous error, and it matches the pattern where "the UI wouldn't let us do that, so how is this value here?" turns out to be an integration write. Confirm the exact enforcement boundary internally before you assert it to a customer — it belongs on your colleague-questions list, and it changes how you design validation for any integrated field.

Concept 4 — The rest of the configuration layer

  • Validation Functions — custom validation, referenceable from a rule via validationFunction.
  • Blueprints — templated structures for repeatable setup (the docs carry a placeholder-colour example).
  • Uniqueness — constraints preventing duplicate values. Worth pairing mentally with Week 4: uniqueness rules and federatedId upsert are different mechanisms answering related questions, and a customer may need both.
  • Option Sets — the value lists behind Single/Multi Select. Recall Week 4: display labels in a CSV are converted to internal option values during preprocessing, so option-set drift breaks loads.

Option Set Hierarchies — cascading dropdowns, and two silent failures

An Option Set Hierarchy (OSH) creates a parent-child cascade between single-select properties: "selecting a parent value narrows the values available in its child dropdown." Choosing Merch Size Group = APPAREL might restrict Merch Division Code to M-APP, W-APP, K-APP. Chains can be two levels or many — Segment → Category → SubCategory — and "the order of the parent → child chain is fixed by the hierarchy list. Changing the order in that list changes which property filters which."

When a user opens a child dropdown the engine finds the child's position in the chain, walks the options tree filtering by the parent values currently selected, and returns what is allowed at that depth. If a parent has no selection yet, it flattens all branches and shows every descendant value.

Two ways an OSH fails without saying so

  1. An empty dropdown. "If the parent holds a value that isn't in the hierarchy, the child dropdown will appear empty." So a legacy or imported parent value that predates the hierarchy — or one loaded straight past the UI — makes the child unusable, and the user reports "the dropdown is broken." It isn't; the parent is off-hierarchy.
  2. A config that silently doesn't load. The hierarchy is stored as an OptionSetHierarchy record plus a TypeOptionSetHierarchyLink attaching it to a Type, and it is keyed on TypePropertyIds — internal IDs, not slugs. "A slug-based config silently fails to load." Someone hand-writing or hand-editing the JSON with readable slugs gets no error and no hierarchy.

Loading one is a two-step CLI flow — parseOptionSetsHierarchyValuesToKeysLocalFile converts display values to keys, then parseOptionSetsHierarchyLocalFile generates the JSON — after which you create the hierarchy in the Admin Console and assign it to the Item and Plan Placeholder types.

Both failures present as user-facing bugs and are neither. "The dropdown is empty for some products" is an off-hierarchy parent value, which usually means data arrived through the Loader that the hierarchy was never told about — a Week 4 problem surfacing in Week 7's territory. Worth adding to your data-model health check: does every parent value in the data exist in the hierarchy?

Concept 5 — Configuration Copy, and how environments actually work

This answers a question that has been open since Week 1: how does configuration move between orgs?

contrail types getAll     # export this org's configuration to the working directory
contrail types loadAll    # import into the destination org

Eleven categories are exported: option set hierarchies · option sets · rule sets · type policies · type property policies · types · rule set links · type option set hierarchy links · type policy links · type property policy links · usage info. Output is a folder tree of .yml files. Identifiers matter: types are keyed by typePath (e.g. item:footwear), while option set hierarchies and policies use identifier.

To promote between environments, you point the configuration at a different org and run loadAll.

Three things follow, and all three are worth saying out loud to a customer early:

  • Configuration is exportable as text, so it can live in version control. Customers who commit their types export get review, history and rollback on their configuration. Recommend this on day one; it is the single cheapest maturity upgrade available to them.
  • It is a whole-org export, not a cherry-pick. Promotion is a bulk operation, so environments drift apart unless promotion is disciplined and one-directional.
  • Configuration Copy moves configuration; apps move separately. Types, rule sets and policies go via types loadAll; workflows and actions go via app publish and install. A "promotion" is therefore two mechanisms that must stay in step — and a mismatch between them is a real and confusing failure mode.

Concept 6 — Apps: identity, ownership, versions

Above the configuration layer, everything ships inside an app. Three facts about app creation are permanent, and you should be in the room when they're decided.

Three irreversible decisions

  1. The identifier is global and immutable. "Creating a contrail app (contrail app create) reserves the app's identifier globally. Since no two apps can share the same identifier, and app identifier can not be altered after creation, it is important to be diligent about choosing the correct identifier." Format is org@domain/app-name.
  2. Ownership is permanent and non-transferable. "Each app is owned by an org. The org that first creates an app is the owner of the app. App ownership can not be modified." Only the owner can modify, publish or delete it.
  3. Publishing creates versions, and installs are what put a version into an org.

Point 2 is a commercial landmine, not a technical footnote. If a systems integrator or VibeIQ PS creates the customer's app in their org, the customer can never own it — not at renewal, not when they change partners, not ever. The remedy after the fact is rebuilding under a new identifier, which also can't reuse the name. Ask "whose org is this app being created in, and is that where it should live in three years?" before anyone types contrail app create. Nobody thanks you for raising it; everyone would have thanked you later.

Visibility is set in app.yml:

visibility: private            # default

visibility: private           # private, shared with named orgs
accessGrants:
  - orgSlug: recipient-org
    permissions:
      - install

visibility: public            # marketplace candidate

Public listing isn't self-serve: "you can submit a request to include your app on the public App Marketplace," which requires VibeIQ approval via the customer success manager. Another lead-time item for an implementation plan.

Installation happens through the Marketplace or the CLI (contrail app install). And from Week 6: publishing turns declared workflows into Workflow Template Definitions on that app version, which an installing org instantiates with contrail workflow-template-definitions install.

Concept 7 — Extensions

Extensions add UI. They ship inside apps and are declared in the manifest:

extensions:
  - identifier: hello-world
    name: Hello World
    userApps: [BOARDS, PLAN]
    display:
      type: modal
      dimensions: {width: 660px, height: 300px}
    iframeUrl: https://example.com
    extensionType: DOCUMENT_AUTOMATION
extensionTypeWhat it does
DOCUMENT_AUTOMATIONReads document context and performs actions on boards, plans and showcases
CONTEXTUAL_ACTIONPresented dynamically based on the user's current context
ADMIN_UTILITYExtends the Admin Console

Display is either a modal — "a centered dialog with a backdrop. Best for focused tasks" — or a side-menu, "a panel docked to the right edge with no backdrop," which lets the user keep working in the document. Side-menu suits inspector-style tools; modal suits a task you start and finish.

The runtime API: AppExtension.registerAppExtension() establishes the iframe connection, getAppContext() reads the current board/plan/showcase and the selected elements, and BoardsApp / PlanApp / ShowcaseApp perform document modifications.

The hosting consequence

"The extension is shown to the user in an iframe" and requires public HTTPS hosting — self-hosted (Netlify, Vercel) or VibeIQ's managed option. So an extension is a service the customer runs: it has uptime, TLS certificates, a deployment process and an on-call owner. When it is down, users see a broken panel inside VibeIQ and will report it as a VibeIQ outage. Establish who owns that hosting before the extension ships, and make sure it is monitored by someone.

Module 8 · Week 8

Platform, security & operations

The conversation you'll have with a customer's IT and security teams — and the one where being honest about what you don't know matters more than sounding complete. This module is deliberately split between what the documentation establishes and what it doesn't, because that boundary is the operational picture.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can (1) describe the org as the tenancy boundary and how environments are actually maintained, (2) walk the layered permission model from org down to individual property, (3) trace where customer data physically travels and where credentials live, (4) say precisely what monitoring exists and over what horizon, and (5) answer a security questionnaire without bluffing — knowing which questions you must take away.

Concept 1 — Tenancy: the org is the boundary

Every authenticated request carries X-Api-Org. Every app is owned by an org. Configuration is exported and imported per org. Concurrency ceilings are per org. The org is the unit of isolation, ownership, configuration and capacity — hold that and most platform questions resolve.

Separate environments are real: the Node migration guide instructs you to "thoroughly test your app in a development org before installing it on production orgs," which confirms multiple orgs per customer as the intended pattern. But note what that implies — an environment is just another org. There is no environment abstraction layered above orgs; dev/staging/prod are a convention the customer maintains by discipline.

From Week 7: promotion is two mechanisms — contrail types getAll/loadAll for configuration, publish/install for apps. Combine that with "an environment is just an org" and you get the real risk: environments drift silently, and nothing in the platform tells you they have. Ask any established customer, "when did you last verify that staging matches production, and how?" The usual honest answer is never, and the usual consequence is a change that tested clean and broke on release.

Concept 2 — The permission model, layer by layer

Access control is layered, and each layer answers a different question:

A correction worth making early

A common first impression — including the one in this program's own Week 1 open questions — is that VibeIQ documents only app-level grants and API-key auth, with no real end-user permission model. That is not right. Type Policies and Type Property Policies exist and are first-class enough to be among the eleven categories Configuration Copy exports. What's missing is documentation, not capability. So the accurate statement to a customer is: "there is a policy-based model with type- and property-level permissions; I'll get you the specifics" — not "permissions are thin."

Still genuinely unknown

How policies map to end users and roles inside Boards, Plan and Showcase is not publicly documented. Whether there are named roles, group membership, or per-project principals — take that away and confirm. Do not improvise an answer to "can we restrict merchandisers to their own division?", because the shape of the answer determines their rollout design.

Concept 3 — Credentials: where they live and how they leak

Three credential surfaces, each with a different exposure:

  • API keys — format app:uEL7GCBZ7Xhd4w2z, app-scoped, long-lived. Docs are explicit: never commit them, use environment variables or secrets management, keep separate keys per environment, prefer app keys over user keys, and rotate regularly.
  • CLI credentials — authentication caches to ~/.vibeiq/configs/. That is a plaintext credential store on every developer and consultant laptop that has ever run the CLI.
  • Webhook trigger URLs — the webhook ID sits in the URL and functions as a bearer credential (Week 5/6).

The CLI cache is the one nobody raises and every security team cares about. When a consultant rolls off, or a laptop is lost, ~/.vibeiq/configs/ still holds working access. Offboarding must include credential revocation, not just removing someone from a channel. And it composes with Week 7's ownership problem: an integrator's engineer may hold both the app ownership and cached credentials to the customer's org. Raise revocation as part of any handover plan.

Concept 4 — Where the data actually goes

  1. Uploads — creating a File entity returns an uploadPost with an S3 URL and form parameters; the binary goes directly to S3 (us-east-1 in the documented examples).
  2. Publish payloads — baseline and delete data are JSON files on S3, reached by links that expire after 24 hours.
  3. Extensions — run in an iframe from the customer's own public HTTPS host, so document context crosses into infrastructure VibeIQ does not operate.
  4. Workflow actions — can POST anywhere the config endpoint points.

Two things a security team will ask that the docs don't answer

  • Data residency. Examples show us-east-1. Whether EU or other regions are available is undocumented. For a European brand this can be a procurement blocker, so find out early rather than at contract stage.
  • Compliance certifications. SOC 2 and similar attestations are not in the public docs. You will be asked for them in the first security review of every enterprise deal.

The 24-hour link expiry is usually presented as an operational constraint (Week 3: store the change ID, not the URL). Reframe it for a security audience and it becomes a control: payload links are short-lived rather than permanent. That is a genuinely good answer to "how long is our product data sitting on a URL?" — and it's the kind of translation that makes you useful in a room full of security people.

Concept 5 — Runtime and platform upgrades

Apps declare their runtime in the manifest: nodeRuntime: "nodejs22.x". Migration from Node 18 to 22 requires updating that field, auditing for deprecated APIs, testing locally on Node 22, and rebuilding native modules via npm install and npm rebuild.

Runtime deprecations are a recurring, schedulable obligation that customers reliably forget, because the app "still works" right up until it doesn't. Every customer with a custom app has a maintenance commitment, and nobody told the person who signs off on budgets. Track which of your accounts have custom apps and what runtime they're on — that list is a quiet early-warning system, and surfacing it before a forced migration is exactly the sort of thing that earns a TAM trust.

The honest gap: nothing here is alerting

Confirm this internally; it is one of the highest-value unknowns on your list, because the remedy (customers building their own monitoring on the process APIs) is something you'd want to recommend at implementation time, not after an incident.

Concept 7 — Capacity as a shared, invisible resource

Recall the numbers: parallelWorkerCount of 1–30 (default 5), 25 concurrent processes per workflow, and 60 per organization — the last adjustable via the Customer Success Manager.

Framed operationally: the org's 60 is a budget shared by every integration, workflow and bulk load the customer runs. There is no per-team allocation and no documented way for a customer to see their own utilisation. So capacity contention is invisible until it manifests as unexplained slowness in something unrelated.

Treat it as capacity planning, and do it before the customer needs it. Maintain a picture of what runs in their org, what it's triggered by, and when it's heaviest — then avoid scheduling bulk loads on top of the nightly integration window. "We moved the load to 03:30 and the sync stopped timing out" is a small win that looks like magic, and it is available to you purely from knowing the ceiling exists.

Module 9 · Week 9

Troubleshooting & escalation methodology

Everything from Weeks 2–8, turned into one repeatable method. This module introduces almost no new facts — its value is entirely in sequence and discipline. The goal is that an unfamiliar problem stops being a puzzle and becomes a route you walk.

Learning objectives

By the end of this week you can (1) localise any failure to one segment of the pipeline before proposing a cause, (2) run the right ladder for that segment from memory, (3) read the platform's evidence rather than reasoning from a customer's description, (4) recognise a class of cause from the shape of a symptom, (5) write an escalation that gets acted on, and (6) hold a customer while it's open.

Concept 1 — Localise before theorising

The single most common failure in technical support is proposing a cause before establishing where the problem is. A theory arrives early, everyone starts testing it, and two days go to a hypothesis that was never constrained by evidence.

The rule

Your first job is not "why is this happening?" It is "which segment of the pipeline is this in?" Only once the segment is fixed do you ask why. Localisation is cheap — the platform records almost everything — and it eliminates whole categories of cause in a single check.

Bisect rather than walking left to right. Start at CP3 — look at the entity itself. If the value is correct there, the problem is downstream (CP4–CP6). If it's wrong there, the problem is upstream (CP1–CP2). One look halves the search space, and CP3 is usually the fastest thing to check because it's just opening the record. Walking the pipeline in order is the instinct; bisecting is the discipline.

Concept 3 — The three ladders

Once localised, run the ladder for that segment. These are consolidated from Weeks 4, 6 and 3/5 — learn them as three, not as scattered notes.

Ladder A — a load went wrong CP2

  1. Get the LoaderProcess ID. Read failureArea first — PARSING_CSV, VALIDATING_DATA or TRANSFORMING_DATA localises it in one field. Then the configuration recorded on it.
  2. Compare that config against the last known-good run — especially if CLI and API runs are mixed.
  3. Read the per-step artefacts loader-process-step-<Step>.json. Diff consecutive steps to find where a value changed.
  4. Check column matching: unmatched columns are ignored silently.
  5. Check option-set alignment: display labels convert to internal values in steps 4/6.
  6. Check federatedId presence and stability — including anything computed in conditionalColumns.
  7. Check the four flags: shouldSkipAssortmentPublish, partialAssortmentUpdate, assortmentSplit, workspaceIdentifier. A "successful" load can be invisible downstream or can have replaced an assortment.
  8. If values are right in the artefacts but wrong in the app, the load is exonerated — move to CP3/CP4.

Ladder B — automation didn't happen CP4

  1. Is there a process at all? Filter the Process List on the entity. None → step 2. Some → step 5.
  2. isActive true, installed in this org, from the expected app version?
  3. Right triggerKey? (assortment has only publish.)
  4. Did the change emit that event? A load emits create, not update.
  5. Which path ran? Read triggerEvent, evaluate conditionals top-down. First match wins; oldData absent on creates.
  6. Did actions succeed? Read taskOutputs. COMPLETE-but-no-op → access grant, or config pointing somewhere harmless.
  7. Intermittent and load-correlated? → concurrency and message grouping, not logic.

Ladder C — it didn't reach the downstream system CP5 · CP6

  1. Did a publish happen? Is there an AssortmentPublishChange? If the change came from a load, check shouldSkipAssortmentPublish — when true no publish event is emitted at all.
  2. Did the Plan rows actually reach the Assortment? (The internal commit is separate from the sync.)
  3. Did the delivery workflow fire? — this is Ladder B, because the integration is a workflow.
  4. Did the consumer receive or poll it? Check their logs, and their last-processed change ID.
  5. Did it process everything — including the delete file?
  6. Any gap in processed change IDs? Missed deletes never replay; re-baseline.

Concept 6 — Escalating well

Escalation is not "I couldn't solve it." It is a handover, and its quality determines whether the next person starts where you stopped or starts again.

What a good escalation contains

  1. The segment. "Localised to CP4 — the workflow fires but the action no-ops." Not "publishing is broken."
  2. Identifiers. Process IDs, LoaderProcess IDs, federatedIds, change IDs, org slug, app version. Anything the next person would otherwise have to ask for.
  3. What you ruled out, and how. This is the part that saves the most time and the part most often omitted.
  4. The shape. Intermittent or consistent, all records or some, since when.
  5. Business impact and clock. Who is blocked, and what deadline it threatens.
  6. Your hypothesis, marked as one. Offer it, don't assert it — a confident wrong theory in an escalation costs more than none.

Point 3 is what separates a TAM from a ticket relay. "I checked the Process List: it fired 47 times, all COMPLETE, taskOutputs empty. Access grants show item: read where the action attempts a write — I believe that's it, but I can't confirm the grant is the blocker from outside" is a handover an engineer can act on in minutes. Forwarding the customer's email is not.

Concept 7 — Holding the customer while it's open

The technical work is half the job. The other half is that a customer with an open incident is anxious, usually accountable to someone else, and judging you on communication rather than diagnosis.

  • Separate what you know from what you suspect, explicitly and every time. "Confirmed: the publish happened. Suspected: their consumer skipped the delete file. Unknown: why it skipped."
  • Commit to a next update time, not a resolution time. "I'll come back by 4pm with what I've found" is keepable. "It'll be fixed today" usually isn't.
  • Give them something to do where you can — preserve an export, pause a load, note affected style numbers. Agency reduces anxiety.
  • Say the impact back to them in their terms: "so this blocks Thursday's line review for the outlet assortment" tells them you understand what actually matters.
  • Close the loop with the mechanism, not just "fixed." The explanation is what stops a repeat and is what they'll repeat to their leadership.

Never answer "who's wrong?" — the question Summit Athletic's PLM lead asked in Week 3. Answer "here's what happened and here's what we change." Adjudicating blame between a customer's teams costs you the neutrality that makes you useful to both, and it is never the question they actually need answered.

Concept 3 — Architecture and workflow review

You did a full one in Week 5 and a workflow one in Week 6. The method, generalised:

  1. Establish the requirement first. Half of bad designs are correct solutions to a misunderstood requirement. Ask what happens if this doesn't run, and who notices.
  2. Trace one record end to end through their design, out loud. Most flaws surface here without any need for cleverness.
  3. Test the unhappy paths — down for a day, duplicate delivery, partial failure, bulk load, the first day of a new season.
  4. Check the irreversible decisions — app ownership, identifiers, property levels, federatedId source.
  5. Name the operational cost — who runs it, who's alerted, what happens when the person who built it leaves.
  6. Rank the findings. Go-live blockers vs. follow-ups. A flat list of twelve problems gets ignored; three blockers get fixed.

The workflow review checklist, specifically

Because supporting customer workflows is a core part of this role, this one is worth knowing cold:

  • Unreachable paths — read top-down, find the first conditional that can always be true. Everything below it is dead.
  • Create vs. update — what does every conditional do when oldData is absent?
  • Trigger fitness — does the event they need actually exist? (assortment has only publish.)
  • Access grants — does every action have write where it writes?
  • Config values — is any endpoint pointing at the wrong environment?
  • Message grouping — what can collide, and is it grouped? What does parallelWorkerCount do under a bulk load?
  • Org budget — what else runs in this org, and when?
  • Failure visibility — who sees a FAILED process, and how soon?
  • Change process — do they understand this is a release, not a setting?

On disagreeing well: lead with what's right in their design, be specific about consequence rather than about correctness, and give the symptom rather than the principle. "This will work until someone's consumer is down overnight, and then Centric keeps styles they've cut — which surfaces in a line review" lands where "you should handle idempotency" does not. You are not trying to win; you are trying to have the change made by someone who still calls you next time.

2 · Failures that produce no error

The dangerous class. Nothing is logged, every status says success, and the damage surfaces weeks later — usually in front of someone senior.

Data model & loading

  • Unmatched CSV columns are ignored. "If the column does not match a key or label on the specified entity type, the row column will be ignored." No warning. Wk 4
  • Step 4 transforms silently. Type transformations run twice — step 4 without warnings so conditional columns can work on clean values, step 6 with them. A value altered in step 4 and overwritten in step 5 never warns. Wk 4
  • No setting nominates the ID column. "There is no configuration to say 'this column is my Federated ID'." Only Custom Entity excepted. Values must arrive in the expected column, or be mapped there. Wk 4
  • Changing federatedIds creates duplicates. "It is critical to ensure that federatedId values in your load files do not change between loads." Nothing errors; you simply get two. Wk 2 · 4
  • Copy-down overwrites hand edits. A Family property reload persists onto every Option. Wk 2
  • shouldSkipAssortmentPublish makes a load invisible downstream. It bypasses the publish workflow, skipping change history, composite assortment updates and the assortment|publish event. The load succeeds, VibeIQ is right, and every consumer stays stale with nothing reporting a problem. Usually set for a legitimate backfill and then left in a reusable config forever. Wk 4
  • partialAssortmentUpdate defaults to false, which means replace. A file containing only the rows that changed leaves the assortment containing only those rows. Reports success. Wk 4
  • A computed federatedId inherits someone else\'s formatting. When a conditionalColumn derives the ID (e.g. "{familyNumber}.split(\'-\')[0]"), a change to that source field\'s format silently changes the upsert key for every row. Wk 4

Publish & integrations

  • Deletes never replay. "Delete data does NOT accumulate across publishes." A consumer down for one publish has permanently missed those deletes. Only re-baselining recovers. Wk 3
  • Polling loses records at the seam. Temporal filtering is not a cursor. Exclusive boundaries drop same-instant writes; in-flight writes commit after the query but timestamp before the cutoff; clock skew skips windows. Wk 5
  • A 404 is not proof of absence. It may mean access denied or wrong entity type. Wk 5

Workflows & configuration

  • Unreachable paths. "Only the first path whose conditional evaluates to true is executed." A broad conditional high in the list makes everything below it dead code. The process still reports success. Wk 6
  • oldData is absent on creates. So {newData.x} !== {oldData.x} behaves differently for new vs. existing rows in the same load. Wk 6
  • Misspelled formula slugs return undefined. "Verify every slug against the live Type before saving a formula." Wk 7
  • Reference data changes don't re-run formulas. "Updates to Reference Data does not cause Formulas to re-run." Correct when written, stale forever after. Wk 7
  • A failed task silently skips the rest. "Only one task runs at a time in a process, and tasks always run in order. If one task fails, none of the subsequent tasks are run." Later actions are never run rather than failed — so never infer they executed from the process existing. Wk 6
  • Deleting a workflow destroys its evidence instantly. "Once deleted, the process list, task list, and logs for a workflow is lost." Disable instead. Wk 6
  • Unmatched assortmentSplit rows are skipped. A new category value or a trailing space in the split column, and those rows quietly never load while the job succeeds. Wk 4
  • An off-hierarchy parent value empties a child dropdown. "If the parent holds a value that isn't in the hierarchy, the child dropdown will appear empty" — usually data loaded past a hierarchy that was never told about it. Wk 4 · 7
  • A slug-based Option Set Hierarchy config "silently fails to load." It is keyed on internal TypePropertyIds, not slugs. Hand-edited readable JSON produces no error and no hierarchy. Wk 7

Platform & operations

  • Environments drift with nothing to tell you. An environment is just another org, and configuration (types loadAll) and apps (publish/install) promote by different mechanisms. Nothing reconciles them. Wk 7 · 8
  • Monitoring is not alerting. Every surface — the workflow dashboard, the process list, LoaderProcess — is something a human must choose to look at. A failed nightly load is discovered by whoever notices missing data. Wk 8
  • Concurrency contention is invisible. The 60-process org budget is shared across every workflow, integration and load, with no documented way for a customer to see their own utilisation. Wk 6 · 8
  • Workflow artifacts vanish with the log. "The artifact will be automatically deleted when the process expires from the log (90 days)." Evidence and attachments die together. Wk 8

4 · Claims to verify before asserting

Say "let me confirm" — not yes, and not no

Three things where the documentation is suggestive but not conclusive. Getting these wrong in front of a customer costs credibility that is expensive to rebuild.

  • Rule set enforcement. Rule sets "evaluate client-side, affecting editor experience for all users." That implies the API and Loader are not bound by them — the working assumption, since assuming enforcement is the more dangerous error. But confirm the exact boundary internally; it decides how validation must be designed for every integrated field. Wk 7
  • Inbound webhook verification. X-Verify-Signature is documented as optional and shown as a shared secret in a header, not an HMAC over the body, while the webhook ID in the URL acts as a bearer credential. Outbound does support HMAC. Keep the directions apart and confirm what is enforced server-side. Wk 5 · 6
  • AI capabilities. Heavily marketed, thinly documented. Named native agents are a public claim, not a verified capability set. Confirm GA vs. beta vs. roadmap before repeating any of it. Wk 1 · 3
  • Customer-facing alerting. Whether customers get proactive alerts on load, publish or workflow failure is undocumented. If the answer is no, recommend they build monitoring on the process APIs at implementation time, not after an incident. Wk 8
  • End-user roles. Type Policies and Type Property Policies exist, but how they map to named roles or groups inside Boards / Plan / Showcase is not public. Never improvise an answer to "can we restrict merchandisers to their own division?" — the shape of that answer determines their rollout design. Wk 8

A correction to make, not repeat

"VibeIQ has no real end-user permission model" is a common first impression — this program's own Week 1 open questions made it — and it is wrong. Type Policies ("access control statements defining READ, CREATE, UPDATE, DELETE permissions") and Type Property Policies ("property-level access restrictions") exist, and are first-class enough to be among the eleven categories Configuration Copy exports. What is missing is documentation, not capability. Say "there is a policy-based model with type- and property-level permissions; I'll get you the specifics." Wk 8

And one figure never to cite

The "15 requests/hour" rate limit circulating online belongs to vibe.co — a different company. VibeIQ's real limits are undisclosed: "rate-limited to ensure system stability," with higher limits available by contacting support. You cannot promise a throughput number, so insist consumers implement exponential backoff instead. Wk 1 · 5

6 · The reframes that change how you think

Ten sentences worth carrying

  1. VibeIQ displaces Miro, Excel and PowerPoint — not PLM. The strongest answer to "isn't this overlap?", because it names what the customer is actually doing today. Wk 1
  2. Publish is not "send to PLM." It is first an internal commit from Plan to Assortment. A publish can succeed while the downstream sync fails — two investigations, two owners. Wk 3
  3. A load is an event storm — unless publish is skipped. Phase 5 upserts, so every bound workflow fires once per row; but shouldSkipAssortmentPublish suppresses assortment|publish specifically, so entity workflows still fire while the outbound integration stays silent. Wk 4
  4. Loads are all-or-nothing. Validation is fail-fast and no partial loads occur — so a rejected file needs fixing and rerunning, not reconciling, and a "completed" load really did apply everything it accepted. Wk 4
  5. The integration is a workflow. Publish events are delivered by an Event Workflow, so integration debugging and workflow debugging are one skill. Wk 5 · 6
  6. The data model is in the payload. The baseline exposes .item, .item.itemFamily and .projectItem — so downstream mapping is a data-model decision, not plumbing. Wk 2 · 5
  7. The valuable answer isn't yes or no — it's "that's possible, and here's what it costs to own." A rule set is a Tuesday afternoon. An extension is a hosted service with an on-call rota. Wk 7
  8. Localise before theorising, and bisect rather than walk. Ask "which segment is this in?" before "why is this happening?" — then check the entity itself first, because it splits the pipeline in half. Wk 9
  9. Never answer "who's wrong?" Answer "here's what happened, and here's what we change." Adjudicating blame between a customer's teams costs the neutrality that makes you useful to both. Wk 3 · 9
  10. "I don't know, I'll find out by Thursday" is a strong answer — when it is specific and it is kept. Bluffing a compliance answer is unrecoverable: security teams verify, and being wrong once means everything else you say gets checked. Wk 8
