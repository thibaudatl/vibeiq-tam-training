First 90 Days and worked examples

Personal sequencing and reference material, deliberately removed from the blueprint. The first ninety days of one person's tenure, the inherited sync-and-performance escalation worked end to end, the map from curriculum week to reusable artefact, and the technical unknowns to close in the first quarter.

Markers

ER = an expert recommendation, not a VibeIQ decision. NEEDS INPUT = a fact this document does not have and will not invent. Both carry through from Document B.

Why this is a separate document

The first ninety days is one person's onboarding plan. Left inside the operating model it makes a function look like a job, and it dates within a quarter while the blueprint does not. The same goes for the worked escalation: it is excellent onboarding material, and sitting in the middle of a section that defines a function it made the whole practice read as an escalation practice. Both live here, and the operating model references them.

Phase 1 · Days 1–30 — inherit and stabilise

The sequence matters more than the speed, and the order below is not arbitrary: each step narrows what the next one has to search. Take it nearly as written.

  1. contrail types getAll — the configuration export, on day one. Ground truth about the configuration rather than anyone's memory of it, taken before anyone's memory becomes the only record. It is also the first entry in the configuration-drift signal, so every later drift measurement dates from this file.
  2. An end-to-end record trace. One record, source system → loader → entity → workflow → publish → downstream consumer. Not a diagram from someone else — the trace, done by hand, watching where it actually goes. This is the exit test for the pre-handoff stage and it is the fastest way to find out which parts of the documented architecture are fiction.
  3. The attribute-ownership audit and the data-model health check (W2). One row per attribute across the three systems, naming which is authoritative, who owns it as a person, and the conflict rule when two disagree. Item layers, property levels, federatedId sources, duplicate detection. This produces the most valuable single artefact the practice has.
  4. Workflow inventory plus the 90-day failure chart (W6). Every production workflow: trigger, path, concurrency and message grouping, named owner, and — the question nobody has asked — who actually sees it when one goes FAILED?
  5. Integration review (W3 / W5) — and take control of the live escalation: localise it first, then set the update rhythm, before attempting any fix. See the worked example below.
  6. Platform and operations (W8): org topology, credentials and where they live, custom apps and which runtime they sit in, concurrency headroom against the ceilings.
  7. The people map. Who decides, who lags, who is sceptical, who has been burned by a previous system. Adoption problems are almost never evenly distributed, and the weak link is a person, not a feature.
  8. Write it up, ranked, and share it. Blockers separated from follow-ups. That document is the credibility, and it is worth more than any fix delivered in the same period.

Days 10–20 · Baseline capture — the step that cannot be done later

Every outcome measure on the scorecard is a reduction or an improvement. Without a number from before the TAM arrived, none of them is provable, and the entire "evidence by end of year one" column becomes an assertion. This step captures those numbers while the evidence still exists.

Then get the customer to agree them in writing. Not approval — agreement that these are the numbers, with the caveats attached. A baseline the customer has not seen becomes a disputed baseline exactly when it starts showing improvement. The 90-day evidence horizon makes this urgent: workflow evidence older than a quarter is gone, and no amount of later effort recovers it.

Phase 2 · Days 31–60 — define and baseline

Phase 1 was discovery, which mostly rewards effort. Phase 2 rewards getting other people to decide things, which is slower and is the actual job. It is stated here as outcomes rather than as a week-by-week list, because the sequencing depends on other people's calendars.

Phase 2 exit criteria and checkpoints the charter approved or decision zero escalated · the health index live · one account plan
What must be true to leave Phase 2
  • The boundary model is approved with a name and a date — or decision zero is formally escalated as blocking it, in writing, with the consequence named. Either outcome closes the phase; neither being true does not.
  • Health index v1 is computed, with its criteria published before the first score. Coarse is acceptable; unpublished criteria are not.
  • The risk register is live, every entry with a named owner, a target date and a closure path.
  • One account plan exists with the technical sections written — not a second plan alongside the CSM's.
  • The weekly leading-indicator review and the monthly snapshot are both running. The snapshot is the non-negotiable one.
  • Hypercare exit criteria agreed and minuted, if the account is in hypercare.
  • Document A has been sent to leadership and every decision-log row has an owner and a date against it — including the rows that are still open.
  • The Support interface session has happened, and the five pull-in triggers are either agreed or the SLA gap that blocks three of them is escalated.
Who confirms
  • Hiring manager — approves the charter, or answers decision zero.
  • Support lead — confirms the pull-in triggers and the queue boundary.
  • CSM — confirms the single-account-plan model and the section ownership.
  • Customer technical lead — receives the first ranked health report.
What is presented, and to whom

Document A to leadership, as a decision request and not as a status update — twenty-one questions with proposed answers, owners and dates. And the first ranked health report to the customer, with the health index and its published criteria attached.

Phase 3 · Days 61–90 — prove and adjust

The test of Phase 3 is not whether the work was done. It is whether anything now exists that would survive this person leaving.

Phase 3 exit criteria and checkpoints a QBR delivered · the escalation closed with a mechanism · the charter revised in public
What must be true to leave Phase 3
  • The full cadence is running — daily sweep, weekly sync and indicator review, monthly pack and snapshot — and has survived at least one week where it was inconvenient.
  • The first QBR is delivered with the standing technical block, containing one risk the customer did not already know about, and it produced dated actions with named owners on both sides.
  • The inherited escalation is closed with a mechanism and a written post-incident note naming the decision that made it possible — or it has a dated plan with a named Engineering owner and an accepted RCA. Closed-because-quiet does not count.
  • Two catalogue services have been run twice and templated, per the templating rule: templated the second time, while the second time was still fresh.
  • The problem-record construct has been used at least once, or it is explicitly recorded that no signature reached three occurrences.
  • The charter has been revised and the revision presented. A role defined once and never revisited was defined wrong, and revising it publicly is how the practice earns the right to keep defining itself.
  • A 12-month roadmap of themes — co-authored with the customer's IT lead, explicitly dependent on VibeIQ roadmap confirmation, and explicitly not a dependency tree. See the note below.
Who confirms
  • CSM and the customer sponsor — confirm the QBR landed and the actions are owned.
  • Customer technical lead — confirms the escalation mechanism holds and co-signs the roadmap themes.
  • Delivery & Support lead — receives the charter revision and the first quarter's exception log.
What is presented, and to whom

The quarterly portfolio review: the charter revision, the first quarter's scorecard with everything that could not yet be measured named as such, the capacity actuals against 50/25/25, and the exception log. If the practice-building 25 % was zero for two of the three months, that is the headline, not a footnote.

Why the roadmap is themes, not a dependency tree

A "12-month technical roadmap with a dependency tree" presumes authority and inputs a ninety-day TAM does not have — the customer's own programme plans, Implementation capacity, and VibeIQ's unreleased roadmap. Neither the job description nor the charter grants that authority. What is deliverable, and useful, is a roadmap of themes: four or five directions co-authored with the customer's IT lead, each with the dependency named rather than resolved, and each explicitly conditional on VibeIQ roadmap confirmation. Publishing a dependency tree built on three unknowns is how a TAM acquires commitments nobody agreed to.

Worked example · the inherited sync and performance escalation

A live synchronisation and performance escalation is the situation being walked into, so it deserves method rather than energy. Week 9 applies directly, and its first rule is the one people break under pressure: the first question is not "why is this happening?" but "which segment of the pipeline is this in?" Localisation is cheap; theorising is expensive, and when you are new it is expensive in credibility too.

This is referenced from Document B's escalation section as the worked example of what "the TAM owns escalated issues through to resolution" looks like in practice.

Localise first, and bisect at CP3

Six checkpoints — source, loader, entities, workflows and formulas, publish, consumer. Start at the entities (CP3): if the value is correct there, the problem is downstream; if it is wrong, it is upstream. One look halves the search space, and it can be done before you understand the customer's business.

Separate three words the customer uses interchangeably slow · late · lossy — different causes, different owners, different fixes
They sayIt meansLook at
SlowThe operation takes a long timeShared capacity, concurrency ceilings, batch size, and "everything is slow" as a symptom shape
LateIt arrives, eventuallyPolling window and interval, queue depth, serialisation via message grouping
LossyIt never arrives at allThe integration seam, unprocessed deletes, silent skips, filters

Different causes, different owners, different fixes — and "the sync is slow" gets used for all three. Ask which one they mean before anything else. Very often the honest answer is "two of them, and we have been treating it as one problem."

The eight synchronisation checks worth running first publish · deletes · partial loads · concurrency — the ones that fail silently

These are the ones that fail silently, which is exactly why a post-implementation escalation reaches a TAM rather than being caught by an alert.

  1. Event-driven or polled? Polling has a seam. The fix is overlap, idempotency and data-derived timestamps — not "last successful run time".
  2. Did a publish actually happen? Is there an AssortmentPublishChange at all?
  3. shouldSkipAssortmentPublish on the loader config bypasses the publish workflow entirely. The load succeeds, and downstream never hears about it.
  4. partialAssortmentUpdate defaults to false, which means replace. A partial file wipes everything not in it.
  5. Are deletes processed by the consumer? Publish deletes do not accumulate across publishes — miss one and it is gone.
  6. Gaps in change IDs mean missed publishes. The recovery is a re-baseline, and baseline links expire in 24 hours (refetching refreshes them).
  7. Intermittent, load-correlated, different records each time is a concurrency race. messageGroupId / dynamicMessageGroupId is the fix.
  8. Duplicates point at federatedId generation, and at records created by hand in the UI that never had one.

The highest-yield question in the whole method

"If you retry, does the same record fail?" Same record → logic or configuration. Different record → timing or concurrency. One question, asked in the first five minutes, eliminates half the possibility space — and it is a question the customer can answer without you.

Evidence has a lifetime

Process List, the Workflows Dashboard and Workflow Artifacts retain 90 days. Baseline links expire in 24 hours. Establish what still exists before promising root cause on a problem that started months ago — and if the trail is gone, say so plainly rather than producing a theory that cannot be tested.

The communication protocol six rules — and why running the escalation well outranks fixing it fast

How an escalation is run matters more to the relationship than how fast it is fixed, and it is the part entirely within the TAM's control.

  1. State known / suspected / unknown separately, every single time. Blurring them is how trust is lost.
  2. Commit to the next update time, never a resolution time.
  3. Always give them something to do. A customer with a task is a participant; one without is an audience.
  4. Say the business impact back in their terms, so they know you understood what it costs them.
  5. Never answer "who's wrong?" Answer "here is what happened and here is what we change."
  6. Close with the mechanism, not "fixed", then write a short post-incident note naming the decision that made the incident possible.

That last note is not ceremony. Repeat-issue rate is on the scorecard, and a post-incident note is the only thing that reliably moves it. It is also the input to a problem record when the same signature turns up on a third account.

Two artefacts are worth singling out. The attribute-ownership map is the most valuable single artefact in the kit — one row per attribute across three systems with a conflict rule — because it turns an argument into a lookup and prevents escalations rather than resolving them. The habit underneath all of this

Bring one risk you found before the customer did, every time. Every weekly sync, every QBR. It is the single habit that separates a technical reference from a well-liked contact, and it is the entire basis of being believed during an incident. And disagree well: lead with what is right in their design, argue consequence rather than correctness, give the symptom they will experience rather than the principle they are violating. You are not winning the argument — you are getting the change made by someone who still calls you next time.
