How to read this

An undefined role is defined by whoever asks first. VibeIQ has not defined most of what follows, so this document is explicit about where each statement comes from.

Provenance markers — read these before anything else

ER marks an expert recommendation: a number, a rule or a structure proposed here, not a decision VibeIQ has taken. Every one of them is arguable and several will be wrong. None of them should be quoted back as policy.

NEEDS INPUT marks a fact this document does not have and will not invent — a Support SLA, a tool name, an ACV band, an approver. The structure around it is written; the fact is a blank with an owner in Document A's open decisions log.

Anything unmarked is stated in the job description or follows directly from it. A blueprint with ten honest gaps is usable; one with ten invented facts is not.

The verb model

Every responsibility in this document resolves to exactly one of five verbs, and everything that is not owns names the accountable party. A responsibility that cannot be expressed this way is not yet defined and belongs in the decisions log instead.

The delivery boundary, stated once

This is a hybrid technical role, so technical depth is correct — but the job description's verbs (design, support, map, transform, validate, load, configure) will be read literally by whoever is short-handed that week. The line this document holds everywhere:

  • The TAM owns discovery, architecture and integration design, the data mapping specification, the security questionnaire response, and technical enablement.
  • The TAM advises on integration build, load execution and production configuration changes — and signs the pre-load checklist.
  • The TAM contributes to migration programmes, roadmap alignment, renewal and expansion.
  • The TAM escalates custom development and platform performance tuning.
  • The TAM does not do pre-sales architecture. This line is protected explicitly in What the TAM does not do.

Where a TAM runs a load because nobody else can, it is logged as an exception in the monthly practice review — named account, named reason, named date. An exception that is never counted becomes the pattern within two quarters.

Four positions, stated explicitly

Four things below are commonly read the other way round, and one of them is a disagreement inside the job description itself. Leaving any of them implicit would give the practice two readings of its own charter, so each is stated here and the rest of this document is written to it.

#The questionThe position
C1 Does the TAM route escalations or own them? "Technical escalation point" is routinely read as the first. Owns. The job description is unambiguous — the TAM is the "primary technical escalation point… owning escalated issues through to resolution in partnership with Engineering and Support". The TAM owns diagnosis, the customer-facing technical narrative, the status cadence, RCA acceptance and the follow-through that stops recurrence. Engineering owns root cause and fix. "Routing" describes nothing in this role, and appears nowhere in this document as though it did.
C2 Who trains the customer? The job description gives "hands-on training for customers" to the TAM, and customer training is usually assumed to be Customer Success's. Both, split by audience rather than by verb. The TAM owns technical enablement of customer IT, data engineering and platform administrators. Customer Success owns end-user functional training — merchandisers, planners, designers. Neither trains the other's audience.
C3 Who owns expansion? The job description asks the TAM to "identify expansion opportunities", which reads as a selling responsibility and is not one. The TAM owns sourcing and qualifying the technical signal. Sales owns the opportunity and the deal. Attribution is recorded at signal creation, in the CRM, which is what makes "expansion opportunities influenced" a countable success measure rather than an argument after the fact. Expansion signals are not a separate discipline: they are watched in technical health and risk and handed over in the Sales section.
C4 The JD header says Department: Customer Success. Its body says the role is "a technical leader within Delivery and Support", and its Why-join-us section refers to a Professional Services team. Not answerable here — this one is VibeIQ's. It is decision zero in Document A. It determines the reporting line, whether the TAM carries delivery capacity, and who owns the escalation queue the TAM is pulled into. Every row of the boundary model below depends on it, so the boundary model is presented as a proposal pending that answer.

Part I

Mandate

What the role is for, where its edges are, and what it refuses. Nothing in Parts II–V is defensible without this part being agreed first.

Mission and value proposition

The mission, in one sentence

The TAM is accountable for whether each customer's VibeIQ implementation keeps producing the outcome it was bought for: decisions get made in VibeIQ, and the context behind them survives downstream.

That is the Week 1 value statement made measurable. It is deliberately anchored to an outcome rather than to a list of activities, because activities change every quarter and the outcome the customer bought does not — and because it is answerable with evidence the platform actually produces.

Value to the customer

  • Their implementation is known, in writing, before it breaks. The difference between a TAM and a very responsive support engineer is that the TAM knows the state of the system on a Tuesday with no ticket open.
  • One technical owner across a seam nobody else spans. A line-planning decision crosses PLM, VibeIQ and a BI consumer. Each system has an owner; the path between them has none. The TAM owns the path.
  • Their own team gets more capable each quarter. Enablement is the only mechanism that reduces inbound at the source; everything else moves inbound around.
  • Escalations are narrated, not managed. Known, suspected and unknown are stated separately, and the next update time is committed to rather than a resolution time.

Value to VibeIQ

  • Renewal risk is dated and written down before the renewal window opens — see the T-90 technical verdict in With Customer Success. A renewal conversation is the wrong place to hear about a technical risk for the first time.
  • Field reality reaches Product in a fixed format rather than as anecdote, and repeat failures stop being tickets and become problem records with owners.
  • Escalation load falls at the source. Enablement, the pre-load checklist and the attribute-ownership map remove classes of failure rather than instances of them.
  • Expansion is sourced from technical signal — a seam being asked about, a second brand mentioned in passing — months before it reaches a CRM.
  • The practice is a reusable asset. Every service in the catalogue is backed by an artefact that already exists, so a second TAM inherits a method rather than a person's memory.

What the TAM does not do

Say this out loud early, because an undefined role gets eaten. Each of the following will be asked for, in a friendly way, within the first quarter. Declining them in the charter is far easier than declining them in the moment, when someone is short-handed and the ask is reasonable.

Nine explicit exclusions

  1. Pre-sales architecture and solution scoping for prospects. The single most important line to protect. A TAM in a pre-sales conversation is being asked to promise on behalf of a system they will later have to operate, with no discovery, no health check and no ability to say no — and every hour spent there comes out of the proactive capacity that is the entire justification for the role. Owner: NEEDS INPUT — Sales Engineering / Solutions.
  2. Building and owning the customer's integrations. Design yes, mapping specification yes, checklist sign-off yes. Writing and maintaining the connector, no.
  3. Acting as the customer's level-one helpdesk. Support owns the queue. The pull-in triggers in With Support are the only doors.
  4. End-user functional training. That is Customer Success. The TAM's audience is IT, data engineering and platform administrators.
  5. Owning the commercial relationship — pricing, contract, licence ceilings, the renewal conversation itself.
  6. Carrying an expansion quota or running the deal. The TAM sources and qualifies technical signal and hands it over. Attribution is recorded at signal creation so that the contribution is countable without the TAM having to sell.
  7. Approving the customer's internal releases.
  8. Being on call for out-of-hours cover, or for code the TAM did not write.
  9. Custom development. Extensions, custom apps and bespoke runtimes are escalated with a cost-of-ownership classification attached, never absorbed.

The exception rule. Any of these done once, under pressure, for a real reason, is an exception — and is logged as one in the monthly practice review with the account, the reason and the date. Three exceptions of the same shape in a quarter is not a run of bad luck; it is a boundary that has moved, and it goes to the quarterly portfolio review as a decision. ER

Part II

Customer operating model

What a customer actually receives, when, and how much of it. Part I says what the role is; this part says what lands on the customer's desk and on what rhythm.

Engagement model and customer lifecycle

The job description scopes the role "from post go-live hypercare through ongoing expansion". An engagement model that starts at "you have inherited an account" misses the two stages where the most damage is done and the most credibility is available. Five stages, each with a trigger, a cadence, deliverables and an exit test.

Why the exit test is separate from the exit criteria

Criteria are things you can check off. The test is a single demonstration that the criteria were real — usually something the customer's own team does unaided. Criteria without a test get met on paper in a bad week. ER

Portfolio, tiers and capacity

The job description says "a defined portfolio of accounts". A practice written for exactly one account does not survive multiplication, and the first thing that breaks is proactive work — because inbound is loud and proactive work is silent. Tiering and a written capacity split are what protect it.

Three tiers

Tier is a function of ACV × technical complexity × integration count, reviewed quarterly with written entry and exit criteria, so that tiering stays a decision rather than becoming a habit.

Capacity — the four rules that protect proactive work

  1. Accounts per TAM. Five to eight Tier 1 accounts at steady state; three to four while the practice is being built, because 25% of the week is going into building it. ER — recalibrate after two quarters of real timings, and argue the change from the numbers in Metrics, never from feeling busy.
  2. The capacity split: 50 / 25 / 25. Half the week proactive (health checks, design reviews, enablement, risk work, the architecture record), a quarter reactive (escalations pulled in under the triggers), a quarter practice-building (playbooks, templates, problem records, mentoring, the operating model itself — four named JD duties). ER The split is the answer to "why can't you just take this one"; without it written down, reactive expands to fill the week and the practice never gets built.
  3. No TAM carries more than one account in hypercare at a time. Hypercare is a daily-cadence commitment. Two of them is not 2× the load, it is the end of all proactive work for both accounts and for everything else in the portfolio.
  4. When two accounts escalate at once, priority goes in this order: (a) customer-declared business impact against a dated seasonal milestone — a line review, an assortment lock, a market week — which beats a severity label, because in this industry a date that cannot move is the real severity; (b) irreversibility — data loss or corruption outranks degradation, because one is recoverable and one is not; (c) blast radius — accounts and users affected; (d) first in. The de-prioritised account gets a named alternative owner and a committed update time within the hour, never silence. The choice and its reason are logged, and repeated collisions are a capacity signal for the quarterly portfolio review.

The QBR, specified

The QBR is where technical work becomes visible to the people who decide on the renewal, so it is specified here rather than assumed.

The three questions a QBR must answer

  1. Is the customer getting the outcome they bought? Measured against the value statement in their own words, not against a generic adoption number.
  2. What is going to go wrong next, and who owns stopping it? The risk register, with dates and owners — including at least one risk they did not already know.
  3. What did we say we would do last time, and did we do it? The dated actions from the previous QBR, closed or explicitly re-committed.

Escalation and problem management

C1 applies here in full. The TAM owns escalated issues through to resolution — diagnosis, the customer-facing technical narrative, the status cadence, RCA acceptance and the follow-through that stops recurrence. Engineering owns root cause and fix. The word "routing" does not describe this role.

What the TAM owns during an escalation

  1. Localisation. Which segment of the pipeline is this in? Six checkpoints; bisect at the entities. One look halves the search space and can be done before you understand the customer's business. Target: a checkpoint named in writing in under one business day. Never open with a theory.
  2. The customer-facing technical narrative. Known, suspected and unknown stated separately, every time. Commit to the next update time, never a resolution time. Always give them something to do. Say the business impact back in their terms. Never answer "who's wrong?" — answer "here is what happened and here is what we change."
  3. The status cadence. First meaningful update within four hours — an acknowledgement is not an update. Then a committed rhythm that is kept. Update-promises-kept is the one number that must never slip.
  4. RCA acceptance. Engineering produces the root cause; the TAM accepts or rejects it on the customer's behalf. An RCA that does not explain the observed symptom is rejected, with the discrepancy named. This is the step most escalation processes leave to nobody.
  5. The follow-through that stops recurrence. Close with the mechanism, not with a status change, then write the post-incident note naming the decision that made the incident possible. Then land the permanent change: a line in the pre-load checklist, a changed default, a documentation fix, a product ask, or an enablement module. An escalation closed without one of those five is closed provisionally.

Metrics and scorecard

One metrics section, not two. The job description's fourteen success measures are the scorecard; the four tiers below are the classification applied to them; the anti-metrics stand permanently beside both.

The metric hierarchy — a causal order, and one rule

technical health → risk → adoption → value → retention / expansion

The rule: a level cannot be reported green while the level below it is red. A green renewal forecast sitting on a red integration error rate is not an optimistic reading, it is an unreported dependency — and it is exactly the failure the T-90 verdict exists to prevent. The order is causal: health produces risk, risk suppresses adoption, adoption produces value, value produces retention and expansion. Reporting up the chain without checking down it is how a practice gets surprised by its own accounts. ER

Time to resolve, made honest five rules that make MTTR ungameable — and never average the two clocks

Measuring time-to-localise instead of time-to-resolve would be more defensible, because resolution frequently runs on Engineering's clock. The job description names time to resolve anyway, and it is right to — it is what the customer lives through. The answer is not to argue it away but to make it a number that cannot be gamed and cannot be unfairly held against the role.

  1. Mechanism in place and confirmed, not ticket-closed. The clock stops when the mechanism is in place and the customer has confirmed the symptom is gone. Closure is administrative and always lags.
  2. Two clocks, kept separate: containment and permanent fix. Time to workaround and time to permanent fix are two different promises. Never average them. One number for both is exactly how "it's fixed" gets said too early.
  3. A stop-clock rule, agreed before it is needed. The clock pauses while blocked on the customer (access, data, a decision they owe) or on a third-party system, and every pause is logged with a timestamp and a reason. Without this agreed in advance, every escalation ends in a negotiation about the number — and a negotiable number has stopped being a measurement.
  4. Report it as a triple, never alone: MTTR beside time-to-localise and update-promises-kept. A long MTTR with one-day localisation and a 100% update record is the job being done well. Fast closes with no localisation and missed updates is not, and no single number distinguishes them.
  5. Split by detection source — proactively found vs. customer-reported. Without the split, getting better at early detection makes MTTR look worse, which punishes the exact behaviour the risk-reduction criterion asks for.

Provisional targets ER: Sev 1 — containment ≤ 1 business day, mechanism ≤ 5 business days. Sev 2 — mechanism ≤ 10 business days. Present them as provisional, set before a single data point exists. Numbers offered as provisional get corrected; numbers offered as commitments get missed.

NPS, and what a TAM can actually move the technical-contact pulse — reported alongside account NPS, both labelled

Taken naively, NPS is the least controllable number on the list: account-level NPS is dominated by end users whose experience is mostly the product. The TAM-moveable portion is not "would you recommend VibeIQ" — it is "would you recommend this TAM", and it is moved by predictive discovery and escalation clarity, not by responsiveness. Four rules.

  1. Add a technical-contact pulse. The IT and data-engineering contacts worked with weekly, surveyed quarterly, with one free-text question: what is the one thing about VibeIQ that still costs you time? The answer is worth more than the score and feeds the improvement pipeline and the field-feedback pack directly.
  2. Report it alongside account NPS, never instead of it, and label both. Two numbers measuring two different things. Presenting the pulse as though it were account NPS is the fastest way to lose both.
  3. Fix the cadence and never move it. No surveys during or immediately after an incident — in either direction, including the tempting week after a good save. A score that moves with survey timing is measuring the survey.
  4. Attribute with mechanisms. Risks found before the customer found them, escalations closed with a mechanism, requests that reached the product. A score with no mechanism story is noise, and claiming it is how a metric becomes a target and stops measuring anything. Every detractor gets a call within five working days, and the theme enters the problem register whether or not it turns out to be technical.

Playbooks, templates and the template standard

The templating rule

Anything done twice gets templated the second time it is done, while the second instance is still fresh. Not at the end of the quarter, not when there is time. The rule has a trigger and a deadline, which is the only reason a documentation habit survives a busy quarter.

The template standard

A template with no standard behind it becomes twelve slightly different documents within a year. Every template carries all six of these ER:

FormatA one-page summary first, detail after. Tables for anything with more than three parallel items. Findings ranked, with blockers separated from follow-ups — every artefact in the kit already does this and the consistency is worth more than any individual layout.
Naming<tier>-<service>-<artefact>-v<n> — e.g. t1-preload-checklist-v2. Predictable enough to find without searching.
StorageThe template library, one location. NEEDS INPUT — which system. A template in someone's drive is not a practice asset.
VersionWhole numbers for changes that alter how it is used, decimals for wording. The version is on the artefact itself, not only in the filename.
Review ownerA named person. For the first year that is the TAM; after that it moves with the service.
Review intervalSix months, or immediately after the second time it fails in the field — whichever comes first. The second field failure is the honest trigger; the calendar is the backstop.

The improvement pipeline

Improvement requests arrive constantly and informally — in meetings, in chat, at the end of unrelated calls. Without a pipeline they become a private mental list the customer cannot see and the TAM cannot defend.

Intake, classification, prioritisation, SLAs, WIP cap and routing five fixed fields · the cost-of-ownership ladder · a WIP cap of three

Intake — five fixed fields. What outcome is wanted · who is blocked · how often it happens · the current workaround and what it costs · what breaks if nothing is done. Refuse to log a request without all five. The discipline itself resolves perhaps a fifth of requests, because the answer to "what breaks if we do nothing" is sometimes "nothing".

Classify on the cost-of-ownership ladder — which is also the routing rule. Push work down the ladder wherever the requirement allows. A meaningful share of "we need a workflow for this" turns out to be a property level, a formula or a rule set, and every rung descended removes a permanent maintenance obligation from the customer. That is the TAM's most under-recognised contribution.

Prioritisation rule ER: rank by (accounts affected × frequency × business impact) ÷ cost-of-ownership rung, descending. Ties break toward whichever item retires a problem record — a fix that closes a pattern beats a fix that closes an instance, every time.

Automation — the rule

Automate the data gathering, never the judgement. The 90-day workflow failure chart, the monthly metrics pack, the release-readiness diff and the configuration drift diff are all mechanical and all currently manual. The ranking of findings, the health index scoring, the RCA acceptance and the customer narrative are judgement and stay judgement. Time the first instance of every service deliberately, so that "efficiency improvements" has a baseline to be measured against rather than a feeling.

Hiring, onboarding, mentoring and career path

The job description makes mentoring, hiring contribution and building a scalable operating model three of its fourteen named success measures. None can be earned by excellent solo work, and waiting for a team before starting guarantees a first year with three blank criteria.

The hiring profile

Written from the actual work: the JD's required experience, plus what the first year proved genuinely mattered, with the disagreements between the two named out loud. The JD asks for seven years and API/JSON/cloud fluency; the first year will show which of those predicted anything. Write both lists and keep them side by side.

Two exercises that beat any résumé screen for this role

1 · The architecture-review exercise. Give the candidate an anonymised integration landscape — a PLM publish path into VibeIQ, a nightly loader feed, a downstream BI consumer polling for assortment changes, with three real defects planted in it. Sixty minutes. Ask for ranked findings with blockers separated from follow-ups. What it reveals: whether they rank, whether they separate blockers from tidiness, whether they notice the delete path, and whether they say "I don't know" about the thing that is genuinely unknowable from the material.

2 · The escalation-narration exercise. Give them a symptom — "the sync is slow" — and ask them to narrate the first thirty minutes out loud: which checkpoint they look at first, which single question they ask the customer, and what they say to the customer at hour one. What it reveals: method versus confidence. A candidate who opens with a theory fails; one who opens with "slow, late or lossy — which do you mean?" and bisects has the method the role runs on. This one question separates method from confidence better than any whiteboard.

Full loop ER: screen · architecture-review exercise · escalation-narration exercise · a stakeholder-communication session in which the findings are presented to a sceptical "customer IT lead" · hiring-manager conversation. The hiring decision belongs to the hiring manager; the TAM owns the profile, the exercises and the scorecard.

The mentoring model

  • A named mentor, not "the team". Weekly thirty minutes, standing.
  • In month one, three artefacts reviewed before they leave the building — anything customer-facing. In month three, review moves to after the fact. In month six, on request. The shift is announced, so ramp progress is visible rather than assumed.
  • The mentor's metric is the mentee's ramp time, not the mentee's ticket volume. A mentor measured on their mentee's output will keep the work. ER
  • The mentee's first assignment is a Tier 2 account, never a hypercare account.

Team shape

At 3 TAMsNo manager. A practice lead carrying a reduced portfolio (two to three Tier 1) who owns the template standard, the monthly practice review and the health index definition. Everyone still carries accounts — a management layer at three is overhead that produces process instead of coverage.
At 8 TAMsA TAM Manager with no portfolio. Two pods of three to four with a Senior TAM in each. One named owner for the enablement curriculum across the portfolio, because by eight it is a product and not a favour. The second-TAM threshold argument becomes a standing capacity model reviewed quarterly.
The threshold, argued in advanceA second TAM is justified when: health-check currency drops below 100% · time-to-first-meaningful-update starts slipping · open unknowns age past 30 days · the practice-building 25% has been zero for two consecutive months · or Tier 1 count exceeds the point where the weekly cadence stops fitting in a week. Set the threshold before it is crossed — a threshold agreed in advance is a plan; the same argument made afterwards is a complaint.
