TAM Practice Charter

The decisions that need sign-off before the Technical Account Management practice can operate: what the role owns, what it refuses, how the portfolio is sized, how it is measured, and the twenty-four questions only VibeIQ can answer. Everything here is extracted from Document B, restated as a decision.

How to read this, in one paragraph

ER marks an expert recommendation — a proposal, not an existing VibeIQ decision. NEEDS INPUT marks a fact this document does not have and will not invent; every one of them appears in the decisions log with a proposed answer and an owner. Anything unmarked comes from the job description or follows directly from it.

An undefined role is defined by whoever asks first. That is the whole reason this document exists and the reason it asks for signatures rather than feedback. Sections 1–6 are what is being proposed; section 7 is what is being asked.

What is being asked for

  1. Answer decision zero — does the TAM report into Customer Success or into Delivery & Support? The job description says both. Every row of the boundary model depends on the answer.
  2. Approve or correct the boundary model in section 2, and sign it with a name and a date. Getting it corrected is the point; being right about it first is not.
  3. Agree the portfolio and capacity model in section 5 — in particular the 50 / 25 / 25 split, which is the only thing protecting proactive work from inbound.
  4. Agree the scorecard in section 6, including the two restated criteria and the anti-metrics.
  5. Assign an owner and a date to every row of the decisions log in section 7.

1 · Mission and value proposition

The mission

The TAM is accountable for whether each customer's VibeIQ implementation keeps producing the outcome it was bought for: decisions get made in VibeIQ, and the context behind them survives downstream.

Value to the customerValue to VibeIQ
Their implementation is known in writing before it breaks — the difference between a TAM and a very responsive support engineer. Renewal risk is dated and written down at T-90, before the renewal window opens. No renewal is surprised by a technical risk the TAM already knew about.
One technical owner across a seam nobody else spans. PLM, VibeIQ and the downstream consumer each have an owner; the path between them has none. Field reality reaches Product in a fixed format rather than as anecdote, and repeat failures become problem records with named owners.
Their own team gets more capable each quarter. Technical enablement is the only mechanism that reduces inbound at the source. Escalation load falls at the source — enablement, the pre-load checklist and the attribute-ownership map remove classes of failure, not instances.
Escalations are narrated, not managed. Known, suspected and unknown stated separately; the next update time committed to, never a resolution time. Expansion is sourced from technical signal months before it reaches a CRM — and a practice that is a reusable asset rather than one person's memory.

Four positions this model takes, because each is commonly read the other way

C1 · Escalation. The TAM owns escalated issues through to resolution — diagnosis, narrative, cadence, RCA acceptance, follow-through — rather than routing them. The job description is explicit about this. Engineering owns root cause and fix.

C2 · Training. Split by audience: TAM owns technical enablement of customer IT, data engineering and platform admins; CS owns end-user functional training.

C3 · Expansion. TAM sources and qualifies the technical signal, recorded in the CRM at creation; Sales owns the opportunity and the deal.

C4 · Reporting line. Not answerable here — it is decision zero.

3 · What the TAM does not do

Nine explicit exclusions

  1. Pre-sales architecture and solution scoping for prospects. The line most worth protecting: a TAM in a pre-sales conversation promises on behalf of a system they will later operate, with no discovery and no ability to decline — and every hour there comes out of the proactive capacity that justifies the role.
  2. Building and owning the customer's integrations.
  3. Acting as the customer's level-one helpdesk.
  4. End-user functional training.
  5. Owning the commercial relationship — pricing, contract, ceilings.
  6. Carrying an expansion quota or running the deal.
  7. Approving the customer's internal releases.
  8. Being on call, or on call for code the TAM did not write.
  9. Custom development.

The exception rule. Any of these done once, under pressure, for a real reason is an exception — logged with account, reason and date in the monthly practice review. Three exceptions of the same shape in a quarter is not bad luck; it is a boundary that has moved, and it comes back to this table as a decision. ER

5 · Portfolio, tiers and capacity

TierEntry criteriaCoverage
Tier 1 · StrategicTop ACV band NEEDS INPUT · 3+ live integrations · custom workflows or extensions in production · multi-brand, multi-region or multi-season data modelNamed TAM, full cadence, every catalogue service
Tier 2 · StandardMid ACV band · 1–2 integrations on standard loader and publish patterns · no extensionsNamed TAM, reduced cadence; escalation on Sev 1 and seam-crossing only
Tier 3 · PooledBelow threshold, or standard configuration with no integration beyond the default publish pathNo named TAM. Playbooks, enablement library, published templates. Support owns everything reactive.

Tier is a function of ACV × technical complexity × integration count, with written entry and exit criteria reviewed quarterly, so tiering stays a decision rather than becoming a habit. The honest promotion trigger for Tier 3 is the first integration crossing an external seam — the point at which nobody owns the path.

Four capacity rules — the ones that need approving

  1. Accounts per TAM: 5–8 Tier 1 at steady state; 3–4 while the practice is being built. ER
  2. Capacity split: 50 % proactive · 25 % reactive · 25 % practice-building. ER This is the single most important number in the document. It is the answer to "why can't you just take this one", and it is what makes the four practice-building duties in the job description achievable rather than aspirational. Without it written down, reactive expands to fill the week.
  3. No TAM carries more than one account in hypercare at a time. Two is not twice the load; it is the end of proactive work across the whole portfolio.
  4. When two accounts escalate at once: customer-declared impact against a dated seasonal milestone first (a line review or assortment lock outranks a severity label, because the date cannot move), then irreversibility, then blast radius, then first-in. The de-prioritised account gets a named alternative owner and a committed update time within the hour, never silence. Repeated collisions are a capacity signal, logged and taken to the quarterly portfolio review.

Two restatements that need agreeing explicitly

"Playbooks established" is proposed as coverage rather than count, and "reusable assets created" as reuse by a non-author rather than count. Both literal phrasings are countable and both reward producing artefacts nobody uses. These are the two rows worth arguing about when the scorecard is signed.

Anti-metrics — what the practice asks not to be measured on

A newly defined role gets measured on whatever is easiest to count, so naming the wrong metrics is part of the proposal.

  • Tickets closed — volume over prevention; a genuinely good quarter looks idle.
  • Response time alone — fast acknowledgements with no content.
  • Usage minutes / logins — confuses presence with decisions being made.
  • Hours logged per account / TAM billable hours — an inverted incentive: it makes a well-run account look like a failure.
  • CSAT on individual tickets — largely measures politeness during an outage.
  • Any activity metric with no outcome tied to it — meetings held, reports sent, checks run.
  • "Scheduled QBR cadence adherence" — satisfied by holding a bad meeting. Replaced by dated actions produced per QBR and closed by the next one.
  • "No escalations last quarter" — possibly excellent, possibly a customer who stopped reporting, possibly underdiagnosis. Absence of an event is not evidence; the audit that ran and the checklist that was adopted are.
  • "The system is working", unqualified — working against what?

Two definitions are non-negotiable and are set out in full in Document B: MTTR keeps the containment clock and the permanent-fix clock separate and never averages them, and the TAM-moveable portion of NPS is "would recommend this TAM", reported alongside account NPS with both labelled — not instead of it.

The honest position in month one

Of the fourteen criteria: nine measurable from month one · three need a baseline captured first (a value statement per account, a first NPS survey, the first instance of each service deliberately timed) · two cannot be scored until the team grows, and for those the deliverable is the artefact, not the number. Roughly 70 % of the health index weight is hand-assembled until the instrumentation asks in section 7 are answered. Saying this before being asked is the difference between a scorecard that survives the year and one abandoned in month four.

7 · Open decisions log

Every unresolved question, each with a proposed answer, an owner, a decision forum and a target date. A ranked list with no addressee is a wish list; the same list with names and dates is a plan. Dates are stated as days from the TAM's start so they survive a change of start date.

Decision zero — answer this one first

Does the TAM report into Customer Success or into Delivery & Support? The job description's header says Department: Customer Success. Its body says the role is "a technical leader within Delivery and Support", and its closing section refers to a Professional Services team.

This is not a naming question. It determines the reporting line, whether the TAM carries delivery capacity (and therefore whether the 50/25/25 split is real), and who owns the escalation queue the TAM is pulled into. Every row of the boundary model in section 2 depends on it, and several rows below cannot be assigned an owner until it is answered.

What happens to a row that is never answered

It is not quietly dropped. At each quarterly portfolio review, every row still open is either answered, re-dated with a reason, or closed by adopting the proposed answer as the operating assumption — recorded as such, with the risk of being wrong stated. An unanswered question that keeps blocking work is worse than a decision taken on incomplete information and labelled as one. ER
