The VibeIQ TAM practice

Three documents that define how Technical Account Management operates at VibeIQ: the decisions leadership has to sign, the blueprint for how the function runs, and the personal ramp plan with its worked examples. Written against the Technical Account Manager job description and the ten-week curriculum behind it.

Document A · proposal to leadership TAM Practice Charter

Mission and value · the boundary model, one row per contested activity with a graded verb and a named accountable party · what the TAM does not do · cross-functional ownership · portfolio and capacity · the KPI charter · and an open decisions log of twenty-four questions with proposed answers, owners and dates.

For the hiring manager, the Delivery & Support lead and the CS lead. Readable and approvable in one sitting.

 Document B · the blueprint TAM Practice Operating Model

Five numbered parts — Mandate · Customer operating model · Technical ownership · Cross-functional operating model · Running and scaling the practice. The engagement lifecycle including hypercare, the service catalogue, the technical health index, the Support boundary, problem records, the scorecard, and the hiring and career model.

For whoever runs or joins the practice. The main deliverable; A and C are derived from it.

 Document C · appendix First 90 Days and worked examples

The three ramp phases stated as exit criteria and stakeholder checkpoints, the day 10–20 baseline capture, the inherited sync-and-performance escalation worked end to end, the module-to-artefact map, and the open technical questions for colleagues.

For the person doing the job. Dates faster than the other two, which is why it is separate.

Why three documents and not one

Because they have three different completion bars, and writing them together makes all three worse. A charter needs to be short enough to approve in one sitting; a blueprint needs to be complete enough that someone else can execute it; a ramp plan belongs to one person and dates within a quarter. Put them in one document and the part that actually needs a signature ends up buried in the middle of it, addressed to nobody — which is the failure mode this split exists to prevent.

The two markers, used throughout

ER — an expert recommendation. A number, a rule or a structure proposed by these documents, not a decision VibeIQ has taken. Every one is arguable, and several will turn out to be wrong. None should be quoted back as policy.

NEEDS INPUT — a fact these documents do not have and will not invent: a Support SLA, a tool name, an ACV band, an approver, an account count. The structure around it is written; the fact is a blank with an owner and a date in Document A's decisions log. A blueprint with honest gaps is usable; one with invented facts is not.

Anything unmarked comes from the job description, or follows directly from it. The technical substance behind it comes from the ten-week curriculum, whose artefacts every catalogue service is built on.

Four positions, stated explicitly

Four things in these documents are commonly read the other way round, and one of them is a disagreement inside the job description itself. Each is stated in one sentence, and A and B are both written to it.

#The questionThe position
C1Does the TAM route escalations, or own them?Owns — the job description is explicit. The TAM owns diagnosis, the customer narrative, the status cadence, RCA acceptance and the follow-through. Engineering owns root cause and fix.
C2Who trains the customer — the TAM or Customer Success?Both, split by audience. TAM owns technical enablement of customer IT, data engineering and platform admins; CS owns end-user functional training.
C3Who owns expansion? The job description asks the TAM to "identify expansion opportunities", which reads as a selling responsibility and is not one.TAM sources and qualifies the technical signal, recorded in the CRM at creation; Sales owns the opportunity and the deal.
C4Which organisation does the TAM report into? The job description header says Department: Customer Success; its body says "a technical leader within Delivery and Support".Not answerable here. It is decision zero: it sets the reporting line, whether the TAM has delivery capacity, and who owns the escalation queue.

Commitments that hold

The material below is the most opinionated in these documents and therefore the first thing a reviewer will instinctively ask to tidy. It costs the author something, which is exactly why it is credible, and it is not traded away for a cleaner scorecard.

Nine things that stay as written the anti-metrics · the instrumentation caveats · MTTR · NPS · the templating rule
  • The anti-metrics tier, in full — including "no escalations last quarter" named as a possible sign of underdiagnosis, "the system is working" as unqualified, activity metrics with no outcome tied to them, and TAM billable hours as an inverted incentive that makes a well-run account look like a failure.
  • The instrumentation discipline — a source, a caveat and a validity horizon for every number, the 90-day workflow dashboard and the 24-hour baseline-link expiry stated as the real constraints they are. Where a signal has no source, that is a finding, not a gap to paper over.
  • MTTR keeps two clocks. Containment and permanent fix are separate promises with a stop-clock rule agreed in advance. They are never averaged.
  • NPS is reported honestly. The TAM-moveable portion is "would recommend this TAM", moved by predictive discovery and escalation clarity rather than responsiveness, and it is reported alongside account NPS with both labelled — never instead of it.
  • The templating rule: anything done twice gets templated the second time it is done, while the second instance is still fresh. A trigger and a deadline are what make a documentation habit survive a busy quarter.
  • The day-one sequence: configuration export before anyone's memory becomes the only record, then an end-to-end record trace, then the attribute-ownership audit and the data-model health check.
  • The attribute-ownership map — one row per attribute across three systems with a stated conflict rule. The most valuable single artefact the practice produces.
  • The relationship habit: bring one risk you found before the customer did, every time.
  • Named, repeatable deliverables rather than availability, as the organising idea of the service catalogue — and every service backed by an artefact that already exists rather than one that is promised.
