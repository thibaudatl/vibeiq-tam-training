The VibeIQ TAM practice

Three documents that define how Technical Account Management operates at VibeIQ: the decisions leadership has to sign, the blueprint for how the function runs, and the personal ramp plan with its worked examples. Rebuilt from the single-page draft against the Technical Account Manager job description.

Document A · proposal to leadership TAM Practice Charter

Mission and value · the boundary model, one row per contested activity with a graded verb and a named accountable party · what the TAM does not do · cross-functional ownership · portfolio and capacity · the KPI charter · and an open decisions log of twenty-four questions with proposed answers, owners and dates.

For the hiring manager, the Delivery & Support lead and the CS lead. Readable and approvable in one sitting.

 Document B · the blueprint TAM Practice Operating Model

Five numbered parts — Mandate · Customer operating model · Technical ownership · Cross-functional operating model · Running and scaling the practice. The engagement lifecycle including hypercare, the service catalogue, the technical health index, the Support boundary, problem records, the scorecard, and the hiring and career model.

For whoever runs or joins the practice. The main deliverable; A and C are derived from it.

 Document C · appendix First 90 Days and worked examples

The three ramp phases stated as exit criteria and stakeholder checkpoints, the day 10–20 baseline capture, the inherited sync-and-performance escalation worked end to end, the module-to-artefact map, and the open technical questions for colleagues.

For the person doing the job. Dates faster than the other two, which is why it is separate.

Why three documents and not one

The original draft was strong on technical substance and weak as an operating model, and the root cause was structural: it interleaved three audiences with three different completion bars in one page. A charter needs to be short enough to approve; a blueprint needs to be complete enough to execute; a ramp plan is one person's and dates within a quarter. Written together they pull against each other, and the charter — the part that actually needs a signature — ends up buried at section 13 addressed to nobody.

The two markers, used throughout

ER — an expert recommendation. A number, a rule or a structure proposed by these documents, not a decision VibeIQ has taken. Every one is arguable, and several will turn out to be wrong. None should be quoted back as policy.

NEEDS INPUT — a fact these documents do not have and will not invent: a Support SLA, a tool name, an ACV band, an approver, an account count. The structure around it is written; the fact is a blank with an owner and a date in Document A's decisions log. A blueprint with honest gaps is usable; one with invented facts is not.

Anything unmarked comes from the job description or is carried forward from the original practice draft, which is preserved at #practice in the training hub — its content is untouched; only a pointer back to this folder has been added at the top of it.

The four contradictions, resolved

Between the draft and the job description, and in one case inside the job description itself. Each is resolved in one sentence, and both A and B are written to the resolution.

#ConflictResolution
C1Escalation routing (draft) vs. owning escalated issues through to resolution (JD)The JD wins. The TAM owns diagnosis, the customer narrative, the status cadence, RCA acceptance and the follow-through. Engineering owns root cause and fix.
C2Training execution assigned to CS (draft) vs. hands-on training assigned to the TAM (JD)Split by audience. TAM owns technical enablement of customer IT, data engineering and platform admins; CS owns end-user functional training.
C3Expansion identification assigned to Sales, while the draft has the TAM spotting signals firstTAM sources and qualifies the technical signal, recorded in the CRM at creation; Sales owns the opportunity and the deal.
C4Department: Customer Success (JD header) vs. "a technical leader within Delivery and Support" (JD body)Not resolvable here. It is decision zero: it sets the reporting line, whether the TAM has delivery capacity, and who owns the escalation queue.

Deleted, and not replaced with a softer version four things that survived the draft by being countable
  • Account health score as "risk indicators identified ÷ total possible". An unknowable denominator, a numerator that rewards finding more risks, and a number that measures the TAM rather than the account. Replaced by the six-signal technical health index.
  • "Scheduled QBR cadence adherence" as a metric. Satisfied by holding a bad meeting — exactly the activity-without-outcome measure the draft's own anti-metrics tier rejects. Replaced by dated actions produced per QBR and closed by the next one.
  • "Zero drift incidents" as year-one evidence. Absence-of-event evidence, the same failure as "no escalations last quarter". The evidence is the audit that ran and the checklist that was adopted.
  • "12-month technical roadmap with dependency tree" as a day 61–90 deliverable. It presumes authority and inputs a ninety-day TAM does not have. Downgraded to a 12-month roadmap of themes, co-authored with the customer's IT lead and explicitly dependent on VibeIQ roadmap confirmation.
Preserved deliberately — the draft's strongest material the material a reviewer instinctively asks to tidy

Carried forward substantially intact, because it is the part that costs the author something and therefore the part that is credible:

  • The anti-metrics tier, including "no escalations last quarter" named as a possible sign of underdiagnosis, "the system is working" as unqualified, activity metrics with no outcome tie, and TAM billable hours as an inverted incentive.
  • The instrumentation discipline — a source, a caveat and a validity horizon for every number, including the 90-day workflow dashboard and the 24-hour baseline link expiry. Where a signal has no source, that is a finding, not a gap.
  • The honest MTTR treatment — containment and permanent-fix clocks kept separate, a stop-clock rule agreed in advance, never averaged.
  • The honest NPS treatment — the TAM-moveable portion is "would recommend this TAM", moved by predictive discovery and escalation clarity rather than responsiveness, reported alongside account NPS with both labelled.
  • The templating rule — anything done twice gets templated the second time it is done, while the second instance is still fresh — and the Tier 1/2/3 classification.
  • The day-one sequence — configuration export before anyone's memory, then an end-to-end record trace, then the attribute-ownership audit and data-model health check.
  • The attribute-ownership map, one row per attribute across three systems with a conflict rule.
  • The relationship habit — bring one risk you found before the customer did, every time.
  • "Named, repeatable deliverables rather than availability" as the organising idea of the service catalogue.
  • Every citation to the Week 1–10 artefacts, mapped explicitly in Document C.
Reworked — nine pieces of existing content the boundary table becomes the spine; the metric sections merge
E1The boundary table is rebuilt with graded verbs and rows for Support, Implementation and the CSM, and placed at the top of both A and B.
E2The service catalogue gains trigger, duration, required inputs, output artefact and who else is involved — then a map to lifecycle stage and tier.
E3The two overlapping metric sections merge into one, anchored on the job description's fourteen criteria, with the four tiers folded in as a classification.
E4A causal metric hierarchy replaces the flat tier list, with one rule — a level cannot be green while the level below it is red — and reporting split by audience.
E5Operating detail added to the cadence rhythms, the improvement pipeline (WIP cap of three), instrumentation (dashboard, owner, manual flags) and template tiering (a six-part template standard).
E6Days 31–90 rewritten as phase exit criteria and stakeholder checkpoints instead of one item per week.
E7The inherited escalation moves to Document C and is referenced from B's escalation section.
E8"Growth, technically led" is dissolved: the signals move into technical health, the handoff into the Sales section.
E9The ask list becomes a decisions log with owners, forums, target dates and status.
